::section 2.6 | Emotional characteristics and motivation
::syllabus Learner's characteristics: characteristics of adolescent and adult learners (academic, social, emotional and cognitive)
::box footprint
{{FP 2.6}}
::endbox
::h Core
A learner is not only a thinking mind. A learner also feels: hope, fear, boredom, pride, shame. These feelings, or emotions (संवेग), decide whether the learner starts a task, how hard she tries and how long she keeps going. The force that starts, directs and sustains behaviour towards a goal is called **motivation** (अभिप्रेरणा). This section explains the main theories of motivation that the examination asks about, and the emotional states, such as anxiety, that help or harm learning.
::sub Intrinsic and extrinsic motivation
**Intrinsic motivation** (आंतरिक अभिप्रेरणा) comes from within the learner. She studies because the subject is interesting, because she is curious, or because doing the task well is satisfying in itself. The reward is inside the activity.
**Extrinsic motivation** (बाह्य अभिप्रेरणा) comes from outside the activity. The learner studies for marks, a prize, a scholarship, praise, a job, or to avoid punishment. The reward lies outside the activity of studying.
A simple test decides the type: ask "would the learner still do it if the outside reward were removed?" If yes, the motivation is intrinsic. A student who studies hard only to win a scholarship is extrinsically motivated. A student who reads history at night because the past fascinates her is intrinsically motivated.
Intrinsic motivation generally leads to deeper and longer-lasting learning. Extrinsic rewards work in the short run, but if they are overused they can weaken a learner's own interest. Going Further explains why.
::table widths=30,35,35
|Point|Intrinsic|Extrinsic|
|Source|Inside the activity: interest, curiosity, satisfaction|Outside the activity: marks, prizes, praise, fear|
|Example|Reading poetry because it moves you|Reading poetry because it is in the examination|
|Effect|Deeper, more lasting learning|Quick results; interest may fade when rewards stop|
::sub Maslow's hierarchy of needs
Abraham Maslow, a humanistic psychologist, arranged human needs in a **hierarchy of needs** (आवश्यकता पदानुक्रम), usually drawn as a pyramid. From the base upwards the five levels are:
1. Physiological needs: food, water, sleep, shelter.
2. Safety needs: security, freedom from danger and fear.
3. Love and belonging needs: friendship, acceptance, being part of a group.
4. Esteem needs: self-respect, recognition, respect from others.
5. Self-actualisation (आत्मसिद्धि): realising one's full potential, becoming all that one can be.
Physiological needs form the base, and self-actualisation stands at the top. The main idea is that lower needs must be reasonably satisfied before higher needs begin to motivate behaviour. A hungry or frightened student finds it hard to attend to lessons. This is the thinking behind giving a mid-day meal to school children, and behind making a college campus safe and welcoming.
Maslow divided the needs into two kinds. The four lower needs (physiological, safety, belonging and esteem) are **deficiency needs**. They arise from a lack, and once they are met they stop motivating; a person who has eaten is no longer driven by hunger. Self-actualisation is a **growth need** (also called a being need). It does not arise from a lack, and it goes on motivating, even growing stronger, as it is satisfied. Note the trap: esteem is the highest of the deficiency needs, but it is still a deficiency need.
::sub Other names that appear beside Maslow
Questions on Maslow often list other motivation theorists as distractors. Learn one line for each.
- **Frederick Herzberg**: two-factor theory of work motivation (hygiene factors and motivators).
- **David McClelland**: theory of achievement motivation (उपलब्धि अभिप्रेरणा); the need for achievement, written n-Ach.
- **Carl Rogers**: humanistic psychology; the teacher as a facilitator of learning.
- **Kurt Lewin**: field theory; studies of leadership styles (autocratic, democratic, laissez-faire).
McClelland's need for achievement is the desire to do things well and to succeed against a standard of excellence. People high in n-Ach prefer tasks of moderate difficulty, where success is possible but not certain, and they want feedback on how they are doing. Very easy tasks give no sense of achievement, and very hard ones offer little chance of success.
::sub Emotional intelligence
**Emotional intelligence** (संवेगात्मक बुद्धि) is the ability to recognise, understand and manage one's own emotions and those of others. The term was introduced by Peter Salovey and John Mayer (1990). It was made popular by Daniel Goleman's book *Emotional Intelligence* (1995). Goleman's components are usually listed as five:
- self-awareness: knowing one's own emotions;
- self-regulation: managing them;
- motivation: using emotions to pursue goals;
- empathy: recognising and understanding the feelings of others;
- social skills: handling relationships well.
Do not confuse this with neighbouring names. Howard Gardner's interpersonal and intrapersonal intelligences (section 2.8) are related ideas but belong to a different theory, the theory of multiple intelligences. L. L. Thurstone proposed primary mental abilities, and Alfred Binet built the first intelligence test. If the question asks who popularised emotional intelligence, the answer is Goleman; if it asks who introduced the term, it is Salovey and Mayer.
::sub Self-efficacy
**Self-efficacy** (आत्म-प्रभावकारिता) is Albert Bandura's term for a person's belief in his or her own capability to succeed at a particular task. It is a belief, not the actual ability. Bandura showed that learners with high self-efficacy choose harder tasks, try harder and persist longer when they meet difficulty, whereas learners who doubt themselves give up early. The belief in one's capability is the mechanism: it influences how much effort is put in and how long it lasts. (Bandura's observational learning is taught in section 2.3.)
::sub Anxiety and performance: the Yerkes-Dodson law
**Anxiety** (दुश्चिंता) is a feeling of worry and tension about what may happen. A little anxiety before an examination is normal and can even help. The relation between arousal (alertness, tension) and performance is described by the **Yerkes-Dodson law**, named after Robert Yerkes and his co-author Dodson (1908).
The law describes an inverted U. As arousal rises from a low level, performance improves; it reaches a peak at a moderate, optimum level; beyond that, more arousal makes performance worse. The optimum is lower for difficult tasks than for easy ones. A bored, sleepy student does poorly; a moderately keyed-up student does best; a panicking student does poorly again.
So two statements are true: moderate anxiety can help performance, and very high anxiety harms it. The statement "performance rises steadily as arousal rises" is false, because it would make extreme panic the best state of all.
::box example
In a B.A. first-year class at a district college in Haryana, Ms Kaur notices that several students from distant villages come without breakfast after a long bus journey and doze in the first period. Reading Maslow, she sees that their physiological needs are unmet. She persuades the college to open the canteen early with low-cost tea and snacks. Next, she makes the class safe for mistakes: no ridicule when an answer is wrong (safety and belonging). She praises specific improvement (esteem). Only then does she introduce an optional reading club for those who want to go further (growth). Before the semester test she tells the class, "Some nervousness is normal; it keeps you alert", and gives a short practice test so that anxiety stays moderate, not overwhelming.
::endbox
::box confused
- Intrinsic and extrinsic motivation: is the reward inside the activity (interest, satisfaction) or outside it (marks, scholarship, fear)?
- Deficiency and growth needs: physiological, safety, belonging and esteem stop motivating once met; self-actualisation keeps motivating. Esteem is the top deficiency need, not a growth need.
- Introduced and popularised emotional intelligence: Salovey and Mayer introduced the term; Goleman popularised it.
- Emotional intelligence and Gardner's personal intelligences: Goleman's EI is a separate theory; interpersonal and intrapersonal intelligences belong to Gardner's multiple intelligences.
- Self-efficacy and self-esteem: self-efficacy is belief in capability for a task; self-esteem is a general feeling of one's worth.
- Yerkes-Dodson: moderate arousal is best (inverted U), not "more is always better".
::endbox
::further
::sub Weiner's attribution theory
Bernard Weiner asked how learners explain their own successes and failures. The cause a learner names is an **attribution** (गुणारोपण). Weiner classified causes along two main dimensions: locus (is the cause inside the person or outside?) and stability (does it stay the same from one occasion to the next, or change?). He later added a third, controllability. The four classic causes fall as follows.
::table widths=30,35,35
|Cause|Locus|Stability|
|Ability|Internal|Stable|
|Effort|Internal|Unstable|
|Task difficulty|External|Stable|
|Luck|External|Unstable|
A student who says "I failed because I did not work hard enough" names effort: internal and unstable. This is the healthiest attribution after failure, because effort can be changed next time. A student who says "I failed because I am not clever" names ability: internal and stable, which leads to hopelessness.
::sub Locus of control and learned helplessness
Julian Rotter's **locus of control** (नियंत्रण-स्थल) is where a person places the control of what happens to him. A learner with an internal locus believes results depend on his own effort and decisions; such learners take responsibility, work harder and persist. A learner with an external locus believes results depend on luck, fate, the teacher's favour or other people.
**Learned helplessness** (अधिगत असहायता), described by Martin Seligman, is the state in which, after repeated failure that one could not control, a person comes to believe that nothing he does makes any difference. He then stops trying, even on tasks at which he could succeed. In students it shows as passivity and giving up. Graded tasks that guarantee early success, and teaching students to attribute results to effort and strategy, help to reverse it.
Do not confuse learned helplessness with: test anxiety (fear that interferes with performance during a test; the student still tries); field dependence (a cognitive style, the tendency to perceive things as a whole and be influenced by surroundings); identity diffusion (one of James Marcia's identity statuses, an adolescent who has neither explored nor committed to an identity; see section 2.1).
::sub Carol Dweck's mindsets
Carol Dweck distinguished two beliefs about ability. In a **fixed mindset** (also called the entity theory), ability is a fixed quantity that cannot be changed. Learners with this belief avoid difficult tasks, to protect their image, and treat failure as proof of low ability. In a **growth mindset** (also called the incremental theory), ability grows with effort and good strategy. These learners welcome challenge and treat failure as information. Dweck found that praising effort and strategy ("you tried a new method and it worked") encourages a growth mindset, while praising intelligence ("you are so clever") encourages a fixed one. Remember: "incremental" is the technical name for growth, the opposite of fixed.
::sub Achievement goals
Achievement goal theory asks what a learner is trying to achieve when she works hard. A **mastery goal** (also called a learning goal) aims to understand and to improve one's own competence. A **performance goal** aims to demonstrate ability compared with others: to be seen as the brightest, or not to look stupid. A **work-avoidance goal** aims to get by with the least effort. Two students may work equally hard; the deciding question is why. Mastery goals go with deeper study strategies and with persistence after failure.
::sub Atkinson and expectancy-value theory
John Atkinson treated achievement behaviour as the result of two opposing motives: the motive to succeed (hope of success) and the motive to avoid failure (fear of failure). He also held that motivation depends on the expectancy of success and the value (incentive) of success.
**Expectancy-value theory** (developed further by Jacquelynne Eccles and colleagues for school learning) states the idea simply: motivation = expectancy of success × value of the goal. Because the two are multiplied, if either is near zero the motivation is near zero. A student who values a course highly but is certain she cannot pass it will make little effort. This is why building confidence matters as much as showing the value of a subject.
::sub Vroom's expectancy theory: a small calculation
Victor Vroom stated a similar idea for work motivation, with three terms:
- **Expectancy**: the belief that effort will lead to good performance (a probability from 0 to 1).
- **Instrumentality**: the belief that good performance will lead to the outcome (0 to 1).
- **Valence**: the value the person places on the outcome.
Motivational force = expectancy × instrumentality × valence.
::box worked
A student is almost sure that effort will bring good marks (expectancy 0.9), fairly sure that good marks will bring a scholarship (instrumentality 0.5), but places no value at all on the scholarship (valence 0).
> Force = 0.9 × 0.5 × 0 = 0
Another student has expectancy 0.8, instrumentality 0.5 and valence 0.9.
> Force = 0.8 × 0.5 × 0.9 = 0.36
The terms are multiplied, not added or averaged. A zero anywhere makes the whole product zero: nobody strives for an outcome that has no value for him, however sure the path.
::endbox
::box slips
- Adding the terms (0.9 + 0.5 + 0 = 1.4) or averaging them (about 0.47). The model multiplies.
- Leaving the zero out of the product (0.9 × 0.5 = 0.45). A zero term cannot be dropped.
::endbox
::sub More needs theories: Herzberg, Alderfer, McClelland
**Herzberg's two-factor theory** (motivation-hygiene theory) came from studies of people at work. **Hygiene factors**, such as salary, job security, supervision, company policy and working conditions, prevent dissatisfaction when they are adequate, but do not by themselves motivate. **Motivators**, such as achievement, recognition, responsibility, the work itself and growth, produce satisfaction and real motivation. For a college: a decent classroom and fair rules prevent complaints; interesting work and recognition make students want to learn.
**Clayton Alderfer's ERG theory** condensed Maslow's five levels into three: existence, relatedness and growth.
**McClelland's acquired-needs theory** names three needs: achievement, affiliation and power. They are learned through experience, not inborn, which is why McClelland held that achievement motivation can be developed through training.
Maslow, too, did not say that a lower need must be completely satisfied before the next appears. He spoke of relative satisfaction: a need must be reasonably satisfied, and a person may be partly satisfied at several levels at once.

::sub Self-determination theory and the overjustification effect
Edward Deci and Richard Ryan's **self-determination theory** holds that intrinsic motivation flourishes when three basic psychological needs are met: **autonomy** (feeling that one acts by choice), **competence** (feeling effective) and **relatedness** (feeling connected to others). Note that "relatedness" also appears in Alderfer's ERG; the deciding word is "autonomy", which only Deci and Ryan use.
Research in this tradition gives a balanced picture of rewards:
- Expected tangible rewards (money, prizes) for a task that is already interesting can weaken intrinsic motivation.
- Unexpected praise that carries information about performance ("your argument in the second paragraph is clear") tends to sustain intrinsic motivation.
- Teaching that supports learners' autonomy (choices, reasons, respect for their views) promotes intrinsic motivation.
- Extrinsic motivation is not always harmful. It is useful for tasks that are not interesting in themselves, and extrinsic reasons that the learner has accepted as his own work well.
The **overjustification effect** is the name for the first point. When people are rewarded for something they already enjoy, they may come to see the reward as their reason for doing it; when the reward stops, their interest falls below its original level. Keep it apart from three other named effects:
- **Hawthorne effect**: people change their behaviour because they know they are being observed or studied.
- **Pygmalion effect**: learners tend to perform in line with what teachers expect of them (expectations become self-fulfilling).
- **Zeigarnik effect**: unfinished tasks are remembered better than finished ones.
::sub Bandura's four sources of self-efficacy
Bandura named four sources from which learners build self-efficacy:
1. **Mastery experiences**: one's own past successes. This is the strongest source, because it gives direct evidence of capability.
2. **Vicarious experiences**: seeing similar others succeed ("if she can, so can I").
3. **Verbal persuasion**: encouragement from a respected person.
4. **Physiological and emotional states**: reading one's own calm or tension as a sign of capability or weakness.
::sub Keller's ARCS model
John Keller's **ARCS model** gives teachers four conditions for motivating learners:
- **Attention**: gain and hold it, for example by opening with a puzzling case or a surprising fact.
- **Relevance**: link the topic to learners' goals, such as their careers.
- **Confidence**: arrange tasks so that learners expect to succeed, for example graded tasks with early success.
- **Satisfaction**: let learners feel good about what they achieved, through rewards, recognition and chances to use what they have learnt.
Confidence is about the expectation of success before and during the task; satisfaction is about the feeling after achievement.

::section 2.7 | Adult learners: andragogy and beyond
::syllabus Learner's characteristics: characteristics of adolescent and adult learners (academic, social, emotional and cognitive)
::box footprint
{{FP 2.7}}
::endbox
::h Core
Most students in a university are adults. Many in distance and evening programmes are working men and women, parents and teachers themselves. The examination asks how adults learn differently from children, and the answer is built almost entirely on one thinker, Malcolm Knowles.
::sub Pedagogy and andragogy
**Pedagogy** (शिक्षाशास्त्र) comes from Greek roots meaning "leading the child". It refers to the teaching of children. In the pedagogical model the teacher decides what is to be learnt, how and when; the learner is dependent and follows.
**Andragogy** (प्रौढ़ शिक्षाशास्त्र) is the art and science of helping adults learn. The word was first used in 1833 by the German teacher Alexander Kapp, but it was the American adult educator **Malcolm Knowles** who popularised it and built a theory on it. Distractors in such questions are usually Piaget (cognitive development), Skinner (operant conditioning) and Bloom (taxonomy of objectives), none of whom is linked with andragogy.
::sub Knowles's assumptions about adult learners
Knowles described the adult learner through a set of assumptions. He began with four and later added two more, so most books now list six. Learn them with their meaning.
1. **Self-concept.** As a person matures, the self-concept moves from dependence towards self-direction. Adults see themselves as responsible for their own decisions, so they resist being treated as dependent recipients of instruction.
2. **Role of experience.** Adults bring a large and varied store of experience. It is their richest resource for learning, so methods that draw on it (discussion, case studies, problem-solving, simulations) work better than one-way transmission.
3. **Readiness to learn.** Adults become ready to learn when the tasks of their social roles, as worker, parent or citizen, demand it. A new clerk becomes ready to learn office software when his job requires it.
4. **Orientation to learning.** Adults want to apply what they learn at once. Their orientation is problem-centred (or life-centred), not subject-centred. Learning organised around subjects for later use is typical of pedagogy.
5. **Motivation.** Adults respond to some external motivators, such as promotion or a better salary, but the strongest motivators are internal: self-esteem, job satisfaction, quality of life and the wish to grow. Outside pressure, fear of punishment and small prizes are typical of school children.
6. **Need to know.** Adults need to know why they should learn something before they set out to learn it.
::table widths=28,36,36
|Assumption|Pedagogy (children)|Andragogy (adults)|
|Self-concept|Dependent learner|Self-directing learner|
|Experience|Of little use; the teacher's experience counts|A rich resource for learning|
|Readiness|Ready when told, by age and grade|Ready when life roles demand|
|Orientation|Subject-centred, for later use|Problem-centred, for immediate use|
|Motivation|Mainly external (marks, praise, fear)|Mainly internal (self-esteem, satisfaction, growth)|
A useful key: readiness is about when adults become ready (life roles); orientation is about how content should be organised (around problems). Students often swap these two.
::sub Methods that suit andragogy
Because adults are self-directing and experienced, Knowles preferred participatory, problem-centred methods: case discussion, problem-solving, projects, role play, peer discussion and learner involvement in planning. Dictation of notes, repetitive drill of definitions and lecture followed by a recall quiz treat learners as dependent receivers of content, so they fit the pedagogical model.
::sub Self-directed learning
**Self-directed learning** (स्व-निर्देशित अधिगम) is, in Knowles's description, a process in which individuals take the initiative, with or without help from others, in diagnosing their learning needs, setting goals, finding resources, choosing and carrying out learning strategies, and evaluating the outcomes. A working professional who identifies what she needs to learn, chooses online resources and judges her own progress is a self-directed learner. Contrast two other terms: incidental learning is unplanned learning that happens by the way; programmed learning follows a path fixed in advance by the programme writer.
::sub Experience: resource and obstacle
Knowles called experience the adult's richest resource. He also saw its other side. Long experience produces fixed habits of mind, biases and assumptions that can close the mind to new ideas. So adults sometimes have to unlearn before they can learn. Any statement that experience is "always" an aid and "never" a hindrance is false.
::sub Each assumption is separate
Knowles's assumptions are separate statements about adults. One does not cause another. For example, "readiness to learn arises from the tasks of social roles" and "adults have a large reservoir of experience" are both true, but the reservoir of experience does not explain readiness. In an Assertion-Reason item built from two different assumptions, the usual answer is that both are true but the Reason does not explain the Assertion. The exception is when the Reason states the very cause given by Knowles, as in "adults resist being treated as dependants" (Assertion) because "the self-concept moves from dependence to self-direction" (Reason).
::box example
At a university's Centre for Distance and Online Education, Dr Yadav teaches a contact class of in-service school teachers for an M.A. in Education. Instead of dictating notes on classroom management, she asks each teacher to describe one discipline problem from his or her own school (experience). The group analyses three of the cases and proposes solutions (problem-centred orientation). She explains at the start why the unit matters for their promotion examination and their daily work (need to know). The teachers choose which two problems to study further (self-concept). A teacher who has just been made headmistress is especially keen, because her new role demands it (readiness).
::endbox
::box confused
- Pedagogy and andragogy: teaching children (dependent, subject-centred, external motivation) against helping adults learn (self-directing, problem-centred, internal motivation).
- Readiness and orientation: readiness is when adults are ready (role demands); orientation is how content is organised (around problems, for immediate use).
- Self-directed and incidental learning: self-directed learning is planned by the learner; incidental learning is unplanned.
- Self-directed and programmed learning: in programmed learning the path is fixed by the programme, not the learner.
- Experience as resource and as obstacle: both are Knowles's view; "never a hindrance" is the trap.
::endbox
::further
::sub Knowles's later view: a continuum
In his early writing Knowles presented andragogy as opposed to pedagogy. Later he treated the two as the ends of a continuum, to be used as the situation requires. Where adults are complete novices in an unfamiliar skill, a teacher-directed approach is suitable at first, moving towards self-direction as competence grows. So andragogy is not a rule that fits every adult in every situation, and pedagogy is not forbidden after the age of eighteen.
::sub Applying the assumptions
Each assumption has a matching implication for teaching:
::table widths=35,65
|Assumption|Implication for the teacher|
|Self-concept (self-directing)|Involve learners in planning their own learning, including diagnosing their needs|
|Experience|Use discussion and case methods that draw on what learners have lived|
|Readiness to learn|Time learning to coincide with the demands of learners' roles|
|Orientation to learning|Organise content around real problems, not subjects|
::sub The learning contract
Knowles recommended the **learning contract** (अधिगम अनुबंध) as a tool of self-directed learning. It is a written agreement, drawn up in advance by the adult learner in consultation with a facilitator, that states the learning objectives, the resources and strategies to be used, the evidence of accomplishment and the criteria by which that evidence will be judged. A portfolio is different: it collects evidence of work already done. The contract looks forward; the portfolio looks back.
::sub Mezirow's transformative learning
Jack Mezirow described **transformative learning** (रूपांतरणकारी अधिगम), in which an adult changes a deep-seated way of seeing. It begins with a **disorienting dilemma**, an experience, often a crisis, that does not fit the learner's existing beliefs. It proceeds through **critical reflection** (आलोचनात्मक चिंतन) on assumptions long taken for granted, and ends in a changed frame of reference, which Mezirow called perspective transformation.
Keep the neighbouring theories apart. David Kolb's experiential learning cycle explains how people learn from experience in general (see section 2.4), without the idea of overturning a frame of reference. Knowles's andragogy concerns assumptions about adult learners. Jean Lave and Etienne Wenger's situated learning concerns learning through participation in communities of practice.
::sub Freire: against banking education
The Brazilian educator Paulo Freire, in *Pedagogy of the Oppressed*, criticised what he called **banking education**, in which the teacher deposits knowledge in passive learners as money is deposited in a bank. He proposed instead education through dialogue, in which teacher and learners investigate problems of their own lives together and become critically conscious of their situation. Freire's work is a classic of adult literacy.
::sub Cross's barriers to adult learning
K. Patricia Cross grouped the barriers that keep adults from learning into three kinds:
- **Situational barriers** arise from a person's circumstances at a given time: job and family duties, shift work, care of children, cost, transport.
- **Institutional barriers** are created by providers: inconvenient schedules, rigid rules, fees, lack of suitable courses.
- **Dispositional barriers** are the learner's own attitudes and self-perceptions: "I am too old to learn", fear of failure.
Ask where the obstacle lies: in the learner's life (situational), in the institution's practice (institutional) or in the learner's mind (dispositional).
::sub Heutagogy and peeragogy
**Heutagogy** (स्व-निर्धारित अधिगम), a term introduced by Stewart Hase and Chris Kenyon, is self-determined learning. The learner decides not only how to learn but what to learn, sets and revises her own goals as the work proceeds, and develops the capability to learn in unfamiliar situations. It emphasises capability and **double-loop learning**, in which the learner questions her own assumptions and goals, not only her methods. In heutagogy the learner, not the teacher, determines the path.
Heutagogy goes a step beyond andragogy. In andragogy the learner is self-directed, but the teacher still largely designs the course and its objectives. Pedagogy, andragogy and heutagogy are therefore often arranged as a continuum of increasing learner autonomy.
**Peeragogy** is learning produced by peers together, without a single teacher at the centre.

::sub What individual differences means
No two learners are alike. They differ in intelligence, aptitude, interests, motivation, background and the speed at which they learn. These variations are called **individual differences** (वैयक्तिक भिन्नताएँ). Recognising them is the reason a teacher varies methods, examples and pace instead of treating the class as one uniform group. The term does not mean that learners prefer to study alone, are taught one at a time, or compete for ranks.
Individual differences are found in every aspect of a person: physical (height, health, energy), mental (intelligence, memory, reasoning), emotional (temperament, anxiety) and social (sociability, leadership). They are differences of degree, that is quantitative (more or less of the same ability), and differences of kind, that is qualitative (different interests, styles, talents). Teaching every student in the same way does not remove them; it usually widens the gaps, because those who are ahead move further ahead and those who are behind fall further behind.
There are two kinds of difference:
- **Inter-individual differences**: differences between one person and another. Ravi is better at mathematics than Sunita.
- **Intra-individual differences**: differences among the abilities of the same person. Sunita is strong in language and weak in mathematics.
Both matter, the second especially in guiding a student towards suitable courses.
::sub Causes: heredity and environment
Individual differences arise from the interaction of **heredity** (वंशानुक्रम), what a person inherits, and **environment** (वातावरण), what a person experiences: family, culture, schooling, nutrition, health and opportunity. Neither alone explains them. Heredity sets a range of possibilities; environment decides where in that range a person ends up.

::sub The first intelligence test: Binet
**Intelligence** (बुद्धि) is the general capacity to learn, reason, solve problems and adapt to new situations. The first workable intelligence scale was produced in France in 1905 by **Alfred Binet** with **Théodore Simon**, to identify children who needed special help in school. It introduced the idea of **mental age** (मानसिक आयु): the level of performance typical of children of a given age. A ten-year-old who does what typical twelve-year-olds do has a mental age of twelve.
The German psychologist William Stern suggested dividing mental age by chronological age. Lewis Terman at Stanford University multiplied the result by 100 and used it in his American revision of Binet's test, the Stanford-Binet. This gave the **intelligence quotient** (बुद्धि-लब्धि), or IQ.
::sub Theories of intelligence
Psychologists disagree on whether intelligence is one ability or many. The examination asks you to match each theory with its author.
- **Charles Spearman: two-factor theory.** Every intellectual task draws on a general factor, **g**, common to all tasks, and a specific factor, **s**, peculiar to that task.
- **L. L. Thurstone: group-factor theory, or primary mental abilities.** Thurstone rejected a single general factor in favour of seven primary mental abilities: verbal comprehension, word fluency, number, space, memory, perceptual speed and reasoning.
- **Godfrey Thomson: sampling theory.** Each task samples a number of small, independent abilities.
- **J. P. Guilford: structure-of-intellect model.** Guilford classified abilities along three dimensions: the **operation** performed (such as cognition, memory, convergent production, divergent production, evaluation), the **content** operated on (such as figural, symbolic, semantic, behavioural) and the **product** that results (such as units, classes, relations). The combinations give 120 or more distinct abilities.
- **Raymond Cattell: fluid and crystallised intelligence.** **Fluid intelligence** (तरल बुद्धि) is the capacity to reason and solve problems in new situations. **Crystallised intelligence** (क्रिस्टलीकृत बुद्धि) is the store of knowledge and skills built up through experience, such as vocabulary and general information.
- **Howard Gardner: multiple intelligences.** See below.
- **Robert Sternberg: triarchic theory.** Three aspects: analytical, creative and practical.
Gardner, Sternberg and Guilford, in their different ways, all rejected the idea of intelligence as one single ability. Spearman is the name linked with g.
::sub Gardner's multiple intelligences
In *Frames of Mind* (1983) Howard Gardner argued that intelligence is not one ability but several relatively independent ones. He proposed seven, and later added an eighth, the naturalistic. This is the theory of **multiple intelligences** (बहु-बुद्धि).
::table widths=30,45,25
|Intelligence|What it is|Who shows it|
|Linguistic|Skill with words, spoken and written|Poet, journalist|
|Logical-mathematical|Reasoning, numbers, logical patterns|Mathematician, scientist|
|Spatial|Perceiving the visual world accurately and transforming it in the mind|Architect, sculptor, navigator, chess player|
|Musical|Sensitivity to pitch, rhythm, melody|Musician, composer|
|Bodily-kinaesthetic|Skilled use of the body and of objects|Dancer, athlete, surgeon|
|Interpersonal|Understanding other people's moods, motives and intentions, and working well with them|Teacher, counsellor, leader|
|Intrapersonal|Understanding oneself: one's own feelings, strengths and goals|Reflective thinker, writer|
|Naturalistic (added later)|Recognising and classifying plants, animals and other features of nature|Botanist, farmer|
Interpersonal and intrapersonal are easily swapped. "Inter" means between people: understanding others. "Intra" means within: understanding oneself. A student who quickly senses classmates' moods and works well in teams shows interpersonal intelligence.
Gardner criticised schools for valuing mainly linguistic and logical-mathematical intelligence. His theory is a reaction against describing a learner by one IQ score.
::sub Sternberg's three intelligences
- **Analytical** intelligence: solving academic problems, analysing, judging. It is what ordinary tests measure.
- **Creative** intelligence: dealing with new situations and producing new ideas.
- **Practical** intelligence: adapting to, shaping and selecting one's everyday environment. This is "street-smartness". A student with average grades who handles real-life situations with unusual skill is strong in practical intelligence.
::sub Guilford: convergent and divergent production
Guilford's two most-asked operations are these. **Convergent production** (अभिसारी चिंतन) seeks the one correct answer, as in most ordinary test items. **Divergent production** (अपसारी चिंतन) generates many different answers and is the basis of creativity. A test that asks "list as many uses of a brick as you can" measures divergent production. Such unusual-uses tests are scored for **fluency** (number of ideas), **flexibility** (variety of kinds of idea) and **originality** (unusualness of ideas).
::sub Cattell and age
Fluid intelligence tends to decline from early adulthood. Crystallised intelligence is maintained, or even grows, with age. So a teacher of fifty may do better on vocabulary and general information than she did at twenty-five, while being slower on novel abstract puzzles. Remember: "fluid" means flexible reasoning on new problems, not knowledge that "flows" from experience.
::sub Calculating IQ: the method
The **ratio IQ** (classical IQ) is:
> IQ = (mental age ÷ chronological age) × 100
**Chronological age** (कालानुक्रमिक आयु) is the actual age since birth. Mental age is the age level of the person's test performance. When the two are equal, IQ = 100, which is average. When mental age is ahead of chronological age, IQ is above 100; when it is behind, IQ is below 100.
Steps:
1. Write mental age (MA) and chronological age (CA) in the same unit, both in years or both in months.
2. Divide MA by CA. MA goes on top.
3. Multiply by 100.
4. To find MA from a known IQ: MA = IQ × CA ÷ 100. To find CA: CA = MA × 100 ÷ IQ.
::box worked
Example 1. MA = 12 years, CA = 10 years.
> IQ = 12 ÷ 10 × 100 = 1.2 × 100 = 120
Example 2. MA = 9 years, CA = 12 years.
> IQ = 9 ÷ 12 × 100 = 0.75 × 100 = 75
Example 3 (years and months). MA = 10 years 6 months, CA = 9 years. Convert to months.
> MA = 10 × 12 + 6 = 126 months; CA = 9 × 12 = 108 months
> IQ = 126 ÷ 108 × 100 = 116.67, about 117
Example 4 (finding MA). A 10-year-old has an IQ of 110.
> MA = 110 × 10 ÷ 100 = 11 years
Example 5 (finding CA). A child with MA 15 years has an IQ of 125.
> CA = 15 × 100 ÷ 125 = 12 years
::endbox
::box slips
- Dividing the wrong way round. With MA 12 and CA 10, 10 ÷ 12 × 100 gives about 83, not 120. Mental age always goes on top.
- Mixing units: dividing 10 years 6 months by 9 years as "10.6 ÷ 9". Six months is 0.5 of a year, not 0.6. Convert both ages to months.
- Forgetting to multiply by 100, and writing an IQ of 1.2.
- Reading IQ 100 as "perfect". It means mental age equals chronological age: average for one's age.
::endbox
::sub Why the ratio IQ fails for adults: the deviation IQ
Mental age stops rising in the late teens, but chronological age keeps increasing. Suppose, for illustration, that a person's mental age levels off at 16. At age 16 the ratio IQ is 16 ÷ 16 × 100 = 100; at 20 it is 16 ÷ 20 × 100 = 80; at 40 it would be 16 ÷ 40 × 100 = 40. The person has not become less intelligent; the formula has broken down.
David Wechsler therefore introduced the **deviation IQ**, used in his adult and children's scales and now in almost all modern tests. A deviation IQ expresses a person's standing relative to others of the same age. Scores in each age group are scaled so that the mean is 100 and the standard deviation is 15.
> Deviation IQ = 100 + 15 × z, where z = (person's score − mean of age group) ÷ SD of age group
::box worked
A 30-year-old scores 65 on a test where adults of her age have a mean of 50 and a standard deviation of 10.
> z = (65 − 50) ÷ 10 = 1.5
> Deviation IQ = 100 + 15 × 1.5 = 122.5
Because IQs are distributed normally with mean 100 and SD 15, about 68 per cent of people have IQs between 85 and 115 (within one SD of the mean), and about 95 per cent between 70 and 130 (within two SD). About 34 per cent lie between 100 and 115 alone.
::endbox
::box example
In a B.Ed. psychology practical at a university in Rajasthan, the class looks at an anonymised case. A boy of 8 years (96 months) has a mental age of 10 years 4 months (124 months) on an individual test. The students compute 124 ÷ 96 × 100 = 129.17, about 129. Then the teacher shows a second case: a woman of 35 whose ratio IQ would look absurdly low, and explains why the test manual gives her a deviation IQ instead. She ends by noting that the same boy may be weak at drawing and strong at music: one IQ number does not describe the whole learner.
::endbox
::box confused
- Inter- and intra-individual differences: between persons, or within one person's own abilities.
- Aptitude and achievement: what one could learn with training (future), or what one has already learnt (past).
- Attitude and aptitude: a settled favourable or unfavourable tendency, or a potential to learn. In Hindi books, अभिवृत्ति is usually attitude and अभिक्षमता aptitude.
- Spearman and Thurstone: one general factor plus specifics (g and s), or seven primary mental abilities.
- Thurstone and Guilford: both proposed many abilities; the three-dimensional operations-contents-products model is Guilford's.
- Fluid and crystallised: reasoning on new problems (declines with age), or stored knowledge (maintained or grows).
- Interpersonal and intrapersonal: understanding others, or understanding oneself.
- Sternberg's creative and practical: dealing with novelty, or adapting to everyday settings ("street-smart").
- Convergent and divergent production: one right answer, or many answers (creativity).
- Ratio and deviation IQ: MA ÷ CA × 100, or standing among same-age peers (mean 100, SD 15).
::endbox
::further
::sub The Flynn effect and other named effects
The **Flynn effect**, documented by James Flynn, is the steady rise in average scores on intelligence tests from one generation to the next, observed in many countries during the twentieth century. It is usually attributed to better nutrition, more schooling and a more stimulating environment. The rise is too fast to be genetic, so it shows that measured intelligence is not fixed by heredity alone.
Keep it apart from three other effects:
- **Matthew effect**: early advantages accumulate over time, so the gap between individuals widens ("the rich get richer"). It concerns widening gaps, not a rise in the average.
- **Pygmalion effect**: learners tend to perform in line with teachers' expectations.
- **Halo effect**: one good (or bad) quality colours the rater's judgement of all other qualities.
::sub The PASS theory
J. P. Das, Jack Naglieri and John Kirby, drawing on the Russian neuropsychologist A. R. Luria's work on the brain, described intelligence as four interdependent cognitive processes. The initials give the name **PASS**:
- **P**lanning: deciding how to solve a problem and checking the solution;
- **A**ttention-arousal: staying alert and focusing on what matters;
- **S**imultaneous processing: combining pieces of information into a whole at once;
- **S**uccessive processing: handling information in a sequence.
The theory underlies a test called the Cognitive Assessment System (CAS). The theory is often asked in Indian examinations.

::section 2.9 | Learners with diverse needs and inclusive teaching
::syllabus Learner's characteristics; individual differences; inclusive education (Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act, 2016)
::box footprint
{{FP 2.9}}
::endbox
::h Core
Section 2.8 showed that learners differ. This section asks what a teacher does about it: how to teach the gifted, the slow learner, the underachiever, the learner with a specific learning disability and the learner with a sensory or physical impairment, all in one institution. The answer the examination looks for is almost always the same: vary the teaching to suit the learner, and change the institution so that every learner can take part.
::sub Gifted learners
**Gifted learners** (प्रतिभाशाली शिक्षार्थी) perform, or can perform, far above their age group in intellectual, creative or other fields. If they are given only the ordinary work they finish early, become bored and may even disturb the class. They need work that stretches them:
- **Enrichment** (संवर्धन): deeper and wider tasks while staying in the same class with age-mates, such as an extra research question or a reading beyond the syllabus.
- **Acceleration** (त्वरण): moving through the curriculum faster than usual, by early entry, skipping a class or grade, or taking advanced courses early.
- Independent projects and mentoring by an expert.
Giving a gifted student more of the same exercises increases boredom; exempting her from work leaves her ability unused; placing her with slow learners for drill wastes it.
**Ability grouping** places learners of similar ability together. **Remediation** (remedial teaching, उपचारात्मक शिक्षण) corrects deficiencies; it is for learners who are behind, not for the gifted.
::sub Slow learners
**Slow learners** (मंद अधिगमकर्ता) have somewhat below-average general ability, and their attainment is in line with it. They can learn, but they need:
- a slower pace and more time;
- simple language and concrete examples;
- repetition and practice;
- frequent encouragement and feedback;
- peer tutoring, in which a classmate helps.
Speeding up, giving fewer examples or withholding feedback widens the gap between them and the rest of the class.
::sub Underachievers
An **underachiever** has adequate or high measured ability but attains far below what that ability predicts. The cause is usually motivational, emotional or environmental: lack of interest, anxiety, family trouble, poor study habits. The key is the gap between potential and performance. A slow learner also gets low marks, but there is no such gap, because his marks match his limited ability.
::sub Specific learning disabilities
A **specific learning disability** (विशिष्ट अधिगम अक्षमता) is a difficulty in one area of learning that occurs despite average or above-average intelligence and adequate teaching. Four are asked again and again:
::table widths=25,45,30
|Term|Main difficulty|Remember by|
|**Dyslexia**|Accurate and fluent word reading, and spelling|"lexis" = word|
|**Dysgraphia**|Handwriting and written expression: letter formation, spacing, speed|"graph" = write|
|**Dyscalculia**|Number and calculation|"calculate"|
|**Dyspraxia**|Planning and coordination of movement|"praxis" = action|
A student who reads well but whose handwriting is illegible, unevenly spaced and very slow shows dysgraphia, not dyslexia. Such learners need extra time, alternative formats (oral answers, typed work) and specific help in the weak area.
::sub Teaching a mixed class
Because learners differ in ability, interest and speed, one method at one pace suits only a few. A teacher meets individual differences by:
- varying methods and pace;
- **graded** (tiered) tasks, so that each learner has an appropriate challenge;
- **flexible grouping**, in which students sometimes work with others of similar level and sometimes in mixed groups;
- extra help for those behind and enrichment for those ahead.
This is the basis of **differentiated instruction** (विभेदित अनुदेशन), developed further in Going Further. Uniform treatment (one common lecture, one pace, identical homework for all) looks fair but ignores real differences in readiness, leaving some bored and others lost. Public ranking after every test harms weaker learners.
::sub Learners with sensory impairments
The rule is simple: send information through the channel the learner can use.
- **Visual impairment** (दृष्टि बाधिता). **Braille** is a system of raised dots read by touch, which lets blind learners read and write. Others use large print, audio material and screen-reader software. On a course web page, **alternative text** (alt text) describes each image in words, so that a screen reader can read it aloud.
- **Hearing impairment** (श्रवण बाधिता). These learners need information through the eyes: **captions** on videos, written outlines of lectures, visual aids, a front seat that allows lip-reading and, where needed, sign language interpretation. Audio recordings and extra oral questions depend on hearing and do not help.
So alt text serves learners with visual impairment, and captions serve learners with hearing impairment. Questions often reverse the pair.
::sub Exclusion, segregation, integration and inclusion
These four words describe how an education system treats learners with disabilities.
::table widths=22,78
|Term|Meaning|
|Exclusion|The learner is denied education altogether.|
|Segregation|The learner is educated in a separate setting, such as a separate special school or class.|
|Integration|The learner is placed in the regular setting but must fit into a system that stays as it is.|
|Inclusion|The institution itself changes, removing barriers and providing accommodation, so that every learner takes part fully.|
A college that admits a wheelchair user to regular classes but keeps her laboratory on a floor she cannot reach is practising integration, not inclusion.
**Inclusive education** (समावेशी शिक्षा) means that learners of all abilities and backgrounds, including those with disabilities, learn together in the same institution, with the supports each needs. It is not admitting only high achievers, separating learners strictly by ability, or teaching everyone by one method.
::sub The Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act, 2016
India's main law on disability is the **Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act, 2016** (RPwD Act). Learn these points.
- It replaced the Persons with Disabilities (Equal Opportunities, Protection of Rights and Full Participation) Act, 1995.
- It raised the number of specified disabilities from 7 under the 1995 Act to **21**, and the Central Government can add more. The 21 include, for example, blindness, low vision, hearing impairment, locomotor disability, intellectual disability, autism spectrum disorder, specific learning disabilities, mental illness, cerebral palsy, dwarfism, acid attack victims, thalassaemia and multiple disabilities.
- A **person with benchmark disability** is one with not less than 40 per cent of a specified disability, as certified.
- The Act defines inclusive education as a system of education in which students with and without disability learn together and the system of teaching and learning is suitably adapted to their different needs.
- It requires **reasonable accommodation** (उचित सुविधा): necessary and appropriate changes and adjustments, without a disproportionate or undue burden, so that persons with disabilities can enjoy their rights equally with others.
- Section 16 asks governments and local authorities to make sure that the educational institutions they fund or recognise provide inclusive education.
- Section 31: every child with benchmark disability between 6 and 18 years has the right to free education.
- Section 32: all government institutions of higher education, and other higher education institutions receiving aid from the government, must reserve **not less than 5 per cent** of seats for persons with benchmark disabilities, and such persons get an **upper age relaxation of 5 years** for admission.
- Section 34: in government employment the reservation is **not less than 4 per cent** of vacancies. Do not mix this 4 per cent with the 5 per cent for higher education seats.
The Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act (RTE Act) is a different law, of 2009.
::box example
At a state university in Punjab, an M.A. English class has 48 students. One is blind, one is hard of hearing, one is a wheelchair user, and two are exceptionally able. The teacher uploads her lecture notes in a format a screen reader can read, adds alt text to every picture and captions to every video clip, and seats the student with hearing impairment in front where he can see her face. The department moves the class from the second floor to the ground floor, because the building has no lift; this is a change in the institution, not in the student, and it is what makes the arrangement inclusion rather than integration. For the two able students she sets an enrichment task, a comparison of two translations of a Tagore poem. A student who is struggling gets a weekly meeting with a peer tutor. At admission the student with benchmark disability got a seat under the 5 per cent reservation for higher education.
::endbox
::box confused
- Enrichment and acceleration: deeper and wider work at the same level, or faster movement through the curriculum (skipping a class, early entry).
- Slow learner and underachiever: low ability with matching attainment, or adequate ability with attainment far below it.
- Slow learner and learning disability: generally below-average ability, or a specific difficulty despite average or higher intelligence.
- Dyslexia, dysgraphia, dyscalculia, dyspraxia: reading, writing, number, movement.
- Alt text and captions: for learners who cannot see, and for learners who cannot hear.
- Integration and inclusion: the learner fits into an unchanged system, or the system changes for the learner.
- 5 per cent and 4 per cent under the RPwD Act: seats in government and aided higher education, and vacancies in government employment.
- RPwD Act 2016 and RTE Act 2009: rights of persons with disabilities, and free and compulsory education for children aged 6 to 14.
::endbox
::box fact
Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act, 2016: 21 specified disabilities; not less than 5 per cent of seats reserved in government and government-aided higher education institutions, with 5 years' upper age relaxation; not less than 4 per cent of vacancies in government employment (checked October 2026). Books written before the Act still give the figures of the 1995 Act: 7 disabilities and 3 per cent reservation. The 2016 Act received assent in December 2016 and came into force in April 2017.
::endbox

::sub Differentiated instruction (Tomlinson)
**Differentiated instruction** is associated with Carol Ann Tomlinson. It means planning varied routes to the same goals according to each learner's **readiness** (current level of knowledge and skill), **interest** and **learning profile** (preferred ways of learning). Tomlinson names four things a teacher can differentiate:
::table widths=30,70
|Element|What varies|
|Content|What is learnt, and the materials through which learners reach it|
|Process|How learners make sense of the content: the activities|
|Product|How learners show what they have learnt|
|Learning environment|The arrangement and climate of the classroom|
If all students read the same passage but may show their understanding through an essay, a concept map or an oral presentation, the teacher is differentiating the product. Direct instruction, programmed instruction and mass instruction do not differentiate in this sense.
::sub Universal Design for Learning
**Universal Design for Learning** (UDL), developed by the organisation CAST in the United States, asks teachers to plan variety into a course from the start, instead of adapting it later for individual learners. It rests on three principles:
- multiple means of **representation**: present content in several ways (text, audio, video, diagrams);
- multiple means of **engagement**: interest and motivate learners in several ways;
- multiple means of **action and expression**: let learners show their learning in several ways.
UDL benefits all learners and reduces the need for later individual accommodation. It is the opposite of one uniform method and one test format for all, of separate special classes, and of waiting until a learner has failed before adapting anything.
::sub Differentiation and UDL compared
Differentiation is mostly a teacher's response to the learners in front of her. UDL is mostly design done before the learners arrive. Both reject the single fixed method. In a question, "planned from the start for all learners" points to UDL; "content, process and product varied by readiness, interest and learning profile" points to differentiation.
