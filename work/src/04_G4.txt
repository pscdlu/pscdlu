::chapter 4 | Methods of Teaching in Institutions of Higher Learning
This chapter is about the ways a college or university teacher can organise learning. It begins with the words used to talk about methods and with the great divide of the syllabus, teacher-centred against learner-centred methods. It then takes the methods group by group: the lecture and its relatives, the group and discussion methods, the activity methods, and finally off-line and on-line teaching, ending with SWAYAM, SWAYAM PRABHA and the other national digital initiatives. The last section is full of dates and figures that change, so read its Fact watch boxes with care.

::sub Four words that are often mixed up
Before we compare methods, we need four words. Examiners sometimes test them, and books use them loosely, so fix their meanings now.
- An **approach** (उपागम) is the broad view of teaching and learning that lies behind everything a teacher does. "Learning is something the learner builds through activity" is an approach.
- A **method of teaching** (शिक्षण विधि) is an organised, step-by-step way of teaching a whole lesson or unit. The lecture, the project method and the seminar are methods.
- A **strategy** (शिक्षण व्यूह रचना) is the overall plan a teacher makes for reaching an objective with a particular group, often combining several methods. "Introduce the topic by lecture, then divide the class into groups, then hold a quiz" is a strategy.
- A **technique** (प्रविधि) is a smaller device used inside a method: asking a question, giving an example, a buzz group of five minutes, writing on the board.
The order from broad to narrow is: approach, then strategy and method, then technique. The safe rule for the examination is that a technique is the smallest unit and an approach is the widest.
::sub Teacher-centred methods
In a **teacher-centred method** (शिक्षक-केंद्रित विधि) the teacher (शिक्षक) is the main actor. The teacher decides the content, controls the pace and does most of the talking or showing. The learners (शिक्षार्थी) mainly listen, watch and take notes. The teacher is a transmitter of information.
Typical teacher-centred methods are the lecture, dictation of notes, the teacher's demonstration and the lecture-cum-demonstration. They are economical: one teacher can cover much content for a large class in little time. Their weakness is that learners stay comparatively passive.
::sub Learner-centred methods
In a **learner-centred method** (शिक्षार्थी-केंद्रित विधि) the learner is the main actor. Learners inquire, discuss, plan, solve problems and make things. The teacher arranges the conditions, designs the tasks, guides and supports. The teacher becomes a **facilitator** (सुगमकर्ता) of learning.
Typical learner-centred methods are the project method, the problem-solving method, group discussion, role play, the case method, the heuristic (discovery) method, peer tutoring and cooperative learning.
Behind learner-centred methods lies a view of learning (अधिगम) called constructivism: knowledge is built (constructed) by the learner through activity, not simply received. The shift from teacher-centred to learner-centred teaching is therefore a shift from transmitting content to helping learners construct knowledge.
::table widths=26,37,37
|Point of comparison|Teacher-centred|Learner-centred|
|Main actor|Teacher|Learner|
|Role of teacher|Transmitter of information, controller of content and pace|Facilitator, designer of tasks, guide|
|Role of learner|Listens, watches, takes notes (passive)|Discovers, discusses, plans, does (active)|
|Communication|Mostly one-way|Many-way: learner to teacher and learner to learner|
|Pace|Same for the whole class, set by the teacher|Varies with learners|
|Assessment|Mostly at the end|Continuous and formative, used to guide learning|
|Relations among learners|Often individual or competitive|Cooperative|
|Strength|Economy of time; covers much content; good for introducing new topics|Deeper understanding, longer retention, skills of inquiry and cooperation|
|Weakness|Little activity, little feedback, no adjustment to individual pace|Slow; needs planning, resources and smaller groups|
|Examples|Lecture, dictation, teacher demonstration|Project, problem-solving, discussion, role play, case method|
::sub Why learner-centred methods are favoured
What learners work out, discuss and apply for themselves is processed more deeply, so it is understood better and retained longer. This is the main reason learner-centred methods are favoured. Note the cause carefully: retention is better because the learner is active. A statement that "in learner-centred methods the learner is passive and the teacher active" is false; it describes teacher-centred methods.
::sub Three cautions that examiners test
1. Learner-centred teaching does not make the teacher unimportant. The teacher's role changes from presenter to designer and guide, and it becomes, if anything, more demanding.
2. Learner-centred teaching uses **formative evaluation** (रचनात्मक मूल्यांकन) a great deal: frequent checks whose results guide the next step of learning.
3. Teacher-centred methods are not "wrong". A clear lecture is often the best way to open an unfamiliar topic or give a large class an overview. The choice of method depends on the objectives, the learners, the content, the size of the class and the time available. There is no single best method.
::sub Inductive and deductive methods: a first look
Two general methods cut across this divide. In the **inductive method** (आगमन विधि) learners study several particular examples and are led to frame the general rule themselves: from examples to rule, from particular to general. In the **deductive method** (निगमन विधि) the teacher states the rule or definition first and learners apply it to examples: from rule to examples, from general to particular. Induction suits discovery and is the more learner-centred; deduction saves time. Section 4.4 compares them more fully.
::sub What the National Education Policy 2020 asks for
The National Education Policy 2020 (NEP 2020) asks all levels of education, higher education included, to move away from rote learning. The pedagogy it wants is described in the policy as experiential, holistic, integrated, inquiry-driven, discovery-oriented, learner-centred, discussion-based, flexible and enjoyable; for higher education it adds freedom for teachers to design their own approaches and greater use of formative assessment. If an option reads "teacher-centred, lecture-driven and examination-based", that is the pattern the policy wants to leave behind.
::box example
In a B.A. Political Science class of 90 students at a government college in Sirsa, the teacher opens the unit on the Panchayati Raj system with a 20-minute lecture giving the structure of the three tiers. That part is teacher-centred, and it is the right choice for a first overview. Then she divides the class into groups of six. Each group studies the records of one nearby gram panchayat, discusses what works and what does not, and reports back. Now the learners are the main actors and she moves from group to group as facilitator. One strategy, two methods, several techniques.
::endbox
::box confused
- **Approach and method.** An approach is the underlying view of learning; a method is the organised way of teaching a lesson. Constructivism is an approach; the project method is a method.
- **Method and technique.** A method organises a whole lesson; a technique is a small device inside it, such as questioning or a buzz group.
- **Teacher demonstration and group discussion.** Both can happen in one lesson, but the demonstration is teacher-centred (learners watch) and the discussion is learner-centred (learners talk and think).
- **Facilitator and transmitter.** Facilitator is the teacher's role in learner-centred methods; transmitter is the role in teacher-centred methods.
- **Inductive and deductive.** Inductive goes from examples to rule (slower, discovery); deductive goes from rule to examples (quicker, practice).
::endbox

::h Core
::sub The lecture method
In the **lecture method** (व्याख्यान विधि) the teacher presents a body of organised content by talking, while the learners listen and take notes. The teacher decides what is said, in what order and at what speed. That is why the lecture is the standard example of a teacher-centred method.
The great strength of the lecture is **economy**. One teacher can address a large class, even several hundred students, at the same time, and can cover much organised content in one hour. The ratio of learners to teacher is high, so the cost and time spent per student are low. This is the correct explanation of the statement "the lecture is economical".
The lecture is useful for:
- giving a large group an overview of a topic in limited time;
- introducing a new topic and showing how its parts fit together;
- presenting recent or scattered material that is not yet in any single textbook;
- covering the syllabus within the time allowed;
- arousing interest, when the speaker is skilled.
Its limitations are the other side of the same coin:
- Communication is mostly **one-way**. Learners listen without much chance to question, discuss or apply, so they remain comparatively passive.
- The lecture gives little or no **feedback** (प्रतिपुष्टि) from every learner, so the teacher cannot tell who has understood.
- It moves at one pace for all and makes no adjustment to individual differences.
- It is weak for developing **psychomotor skills** (manual and practical skills), because skills need practice by the learner, and in a lecture the learner only listens. Note the cause: the lecture is weak for skills because there is no learner practice, not because the class is large.
- Attention falls as the lecture goes on.
::sub Attention and the "fifteen to twenty minutes" advice
Attention is high at the start of a lecture. It is commonly said to decline after about fifteen to twenty minutes. Lecturers are therefore advised to divide the hour into segments and to break it with a question, a short task, an example, a visual aid or a change of medium. This advice is found in most textbooks and is what examination questions expect. You should know, however, that the research behind the exact figure is disputed: some psychologists (for example Wilson and Korn, 2007) have argued that the evidence for a fixed attention span of this length is weak. The safe answer in the examination remains "about fifteen to twenty minutes".
::sub Making a lecture more effective
A lecture improves when it becomes partly two-way. The teacher should:
- state the plan of the lecture at the start and sum up at the end;
- pause to ask questions and invite questions;
- give examples from the learners' own experience;
- use the board, slides, charts or objects at intervals;
- insert short activities, such as a two-minute discussion with a neighbour;
- keep eye contact and speak at a pace the class can follow.
Speaking very fast to cover more, reading straight from the book and avoiding eye contact all make a lecture worse.
::sub The lecture as communication
In terms of the communication process, a lecture has a **sender** (the teacher), a **message** (the content, encoded in words), a **channel** (voice, board, slides) and **receivers** (the students). What it largely lacks is **feedback**, the return flow from the receivers to the sender. Without it, the lecturer cannot tell whether the message has been understood as intended. Questions, pauses and short tasks restore the feedback loop.
::sub The demonstration method
In the **demonstration method** (प्रदर्शन विधि) the teacher shows, step by step, how something is done, while explaining what is being done and why. Typical demonstrations are a laboratory experiment, the use of an instrument, a dissection, a surveying technique, a computer procedure, or a dance step. It suits practical skills and procedures, because learners see the process before trying it themselves.
For a demonstration to work:
- every student must be able to see and hear each step clearly;
- the teacher must rehearse it beforehand;
- the apparatus should be simple and the steps taken at a moderate pace;
- the teacher should question the class during the demonstration.
The best way to keep students thinking is to ask them to **predict** what will happen, **observe** it, and then **explain** it. Announcing the conclusion before starting, performing silently, or letting only the front rows see, all leave students passive.
A demonstration is more concrete than a lecture, because learners see as well as hear. But it is still teacher-centred: the teacher performs and the learners watch. It does not give hands-on practice. A manipulative skill, such as titration, is learnt only when the demonstration is followed by **laboratory practice** by each learner, with feedback.
::sub Lecture-cum-demonstration
In the **lecture-cum-demonstration method** the teacher explains a principle and at the same time demonstrates it with apparatus, specimens or models. It combines telling and showing, so learners both hear and see. It is widely used in science teaching in Indian colleges. It is more concrete than a pure lecture, though the learners still mostly watch.
::sub Matching method to objective
The method should follow the objective (उद्देश्य), the size of the class and the time. For an overview of a policy document for 180 students in one period, the lecture is suitable. For the skill of titration for 20 students, a demonstration must be followed by laboratory practice. Watching alone does not give a skill.
::sub Programmed instruction
**Programmed instruction** (अभिक्रमित अनुदेशन) is a form of self-instruction. The content is broken into a sequence of small units called **frames**. Each frame gives a small piece of information and asks the learner to respond, for example by filling in a blank. The learner then checks the answer at once and moves to the next frame. The sequence of frames is called a **programme**. It can be printed as a programmed text or presented by a teaching machine or a computer.
Programmed instruction is based on the operant conditioning (क्रियाप्रसूत अनुबंधन) of **B. F. Skinner**. In operant conditioning, behaviour that is followed by reinforcement (पुनर्बलन) becomes more likely. In a programme, the immediate knowledge that one's response is correct acts as the reinforcement.
Its principles are usually listed as five:
1. **Small steps.** Content is broken into small frames.
2. **Active responding.** The learner must respond at every frame, not just read.
3. **Immediate confirmation** (or immediate feedback, knowledge of results). The learner knows at once whether the response is right. This reinforces right answers and prevents wrong ones from being practised.
4. **Self-pacing.** Each learner works alone, quickly or slowly as needed. A pace fixed by the teacher for the whole class is the mark of conventional teaching, not of programmed instruction.
5. **Student testing** (also called learner verification or evaluation). The programme is tried out on learners and revised wherever they make errors; the learner's record shows how well the programme works.
If a learner checks each answer immediately after writing it, the principle at work is immediate confirmation. If a question asks which principle allows each learner to go at his or her own speed, the answer is self-pacing.
::sub Linear and branching programmes
There are two main styles of programme.
In **linear programming** (रेखीय अभिक्रमण), developed by Skinner, every learner passes through the same fixed sequence of small frames, one after another. The steps are kept very small so that the learner's responses are almost always correct and therefore reinforced; errors are kept to a minimum. Responses are **constructed**: the learner writes the answer. Only the pace varies from learner to learner; the path is the same for all.
In **branching programming** (शाखीय अभिक्रमण), developed by **Norman A. Crowder**, frames are larger. Each frame ends with a **multiple-choice** question. The choice the learner makes decides the next frame. A correct choice takes the learner forward; a wrong choice leads to a **remedial frame** that explains the error and then returns the learner to the main sequence. Errors are not avoided; they are used for diagnosis. Crowder called this intrinsic programming. Because the route depends on each learner's responses, both the path and the pace are individualised, so branching adjusts to individual differences better than linear programming.
::table widths=28,36,36
|Feature|Linear (Skinner)|Branching (Crowder)|
|Size of frame|Small|Larger|
|Type of response|Constructed (learner writes the answer)|Multiple-choice (learner selects)|
|Errors|Kept to a minimum|Used for diagnosis; lead to remedial frames|
|Path|Same for all learners|Depends on each learner's response|
|What varies|Pace only|Pace and path|
|Basis|Operant conditioning, reinforcement of correct responses|Diagnosis and correction of errors|
::box example
In a B.Sc. Chemistry class of 60 at a college in Hisar, the teacher first gives a 15-minute lecture on acid-base titration. Then, at the front bench raised on a platform so that all can see, she performs a titration, stopping before each step to ask, "What colour do you expect now?" Students write a prediction, watch, and explain. Next week, each pair of students performs the titration in the laboratory while she moves from bench to bench. For students who missed the class, the department has a programmed booklet on titration calculations: each page gives one small step, a blank to fill and the answer overleaf.
::endbox
::box confused
- **Lecture and demonstration.** Both are teacher-centred. In a lecture learners hear; in a demonstration they also see a process performed. Neither gives hands-on practice.
- **Demonstration and laboratory practice.** In a demonstration the teacher performs; in laboratory practice each learner performs. Skills come from practice.
- **Economy and participation.** The lecture is economical (many learners per teacher, much content per hour); it is not participatory. An answer that the lecture "ensures active participation" is false.
- **Immediate confirmation and self-pacing.** Immediate confirmation is knowing at once whether the response is right; self-pacing is going at one's own speed.
- **Linear and branching.** Linear: small frames, written responses, minimal errors, same path (Skinner). Branching: larger frames, multiple choice, errors lead to remedial frames, path depends on response (Crowder).
::endbox
::further
::sub Why the five principles hang together
Each principle of programmed instruction follows from operant conditioning. Small steps make correct responses likely. Active responding produces the behaviour to be reinforced. Immediate confirmation is the reinforcement. Self-pacing lets each learner respond only when ready. Student testing makes sure that the steps really are small enough for real learners. In an Assertion-Reason item, "programmed instruction allows learners to proceed at their own pace" is true, while "in programmed instruction the pace is fixed by the teacher for the whole class" is false.
::sub Mathetics
A third style of programming, **mathetics**, was developed by Thomas F. Gilbert. It uses **retrogressive chaining**: a chain of steps is taught backwards, so that the final step is mastered first, then the step before it, and so on. In this way the learner always ends a practice by completing the whole task. If a matching item asks for "the final step is mastered first, working backwards", the answer is mathetics. The other self-paced plan often listed with programmed instruction, the Personalised System of Instruction (the Keller Plan), is taught in section 4.4.
::sub When the lecture still wins
Two true statements are often paired in a way that tempts you to link them. "The lecture is suitable for introducing a new topic to a large class" is true because of economy, not because of participation. "The lecture is weak for skills" is true because the learner does not practise, not because the class is large. Read every Reason and ask whether it gives the cause.

::endbox
::h Core
Group methods bring learners together so that they talk, question and work with one another and with experts. Most of them are learner-centred or at least interactive. Examiners test them mainly by asking you to tell one format from another, so for each method learn the one feature that decides it.
::sub The tutorial
A **tutorial** (अनुशिक्षण) is a meeting of a tutor with a small group of students, or with one or two students. The tutor clears doubts, checks understanding, discusses written work and gives personal feedback. Its function is mainly **remedial**: it deals with individual and small-group difficulties that a lecture to a large class cannot reach. When a tutor finds that six students share one misconception and gives them exercises aimed at it, the tutorial is serving a remedial function, supported by diagnosis. Nothing is being graded (summative), placed or predicted (prognostic).
Tutorials **supplement** lectures; they do not replace them. A lecture addresses a large group; a tutorial addresses a small group and its particular problems.
::sub Peer tutoring
In **peer tutoring** (सहपाठी शिक्षण) students teach fellow students, usually a more advanced student helping a less advanced one. It benefits the tutor as well as the tutee: to explain something, the tutor has to organise it and understand it more deeply. Do not confuse peer tutoring (students as teachers) with team teaching (several teachers working together).
::sub The seminar
A **seminar** (संगोष्ठी) centres on a prepared paper. A student or scholar presents a paper on a topic, and the group then discusses and questions it in depth, under a **chairperson**. A seminar develops skills of study, presentation, argument and criticism. The usual order of a seminar session is:
1. The chairperson introduces the theme and the speaker.
2. The paper is presented.
3. Participants discuss it and raise questions; the speaker responds. Often a designated **discussant** first comments critically on the paper.
4. The chairperson sums up and closes the session.
Summing up comes last, because the chairperson must take account of the points raised in the discussion. The defining feature of a seminar is one paper (or a few) examined in depth.
::sub The symposium
A **symposium** (परिसंवाद) is a series of short, prepared talks by several speakers, each dealing with a **different aspect** of one theme, one after another. The speakers do not converse with one another. At the end, the chairperson usually invites questions from the audience.
A symposium gives the audience a many-sided view of a topic in a short time. Note the cause: the breadth comes from having several speakers on different aspects. The question session at the end is customary, but it is not what produces the breadth. And questions from the audience are not prohibited; they normally close a symposium.
::sub The panel discussion
In a **panel discussion** (पैनल चर्चा) a small group of experts, usually three to six, sit before an audience and **converse informally among themselves** on a topic, without prepared speeches, guided by a **moderator**. Questions from the audience usually follow.
The moderator introduces the topic and the panel, puts questions to the members, gives each a fair chance to speak, prevents digression and sums up.
::sub Symposium or panel: the deciding difference
Both have several experts and an audience. Ask: do the speakers present or converse?
- Prepared statements on different aspects, delivered one after another, without conversation: **symposium**.
- Spontaneous exchange of views among the experts, under a moderator: **panel discussion**.
The same four specialists can do either. If they set out four positions in turn, it is a symposium; if they challenge one another freely before the students, it is a panel.
::sub The workshop
A **workshop** (कार्यशाला) is a working session in which participants learn by doing. They practise a skill or produce something, such as a question bank, test items, a research proposal or teaching materials, under the guidance of **resource persons**. Talks by experts are short and serve the work. The defining mark of a workshop is practical work by the participants leading to a product.
::sub The conference
A **conference** (सम्मेलन) is a larger gathering on a broad theme. It usually has plenary lectures (to all participants) and many parallel sessions in which papers are presented, and it may run over several days. A conference involves more participants and a broader theme than a seminar, but both include the presentation of papers. A statement that a conference has no place for papers is false.
::sub The debate
A **debate** (वाद-विवाद) has a **motion** (a stated proposition), two opposing sides (for and against), a fixed order of speakers and time limits. It trains students to gather evidence and argue logically. In a panel, members exchange views without taking fixed opposing sides. A **colloquium** is simply an academic meeting at which scholars discuss a subject, often after a talk; it has no motion and no opposing teams.

::sub Brainstorming
**Brainstorming** (मस्तिष्क उद्वेलन) is a technique for producing as many ideas as possible on a problem, in a group, without criticism at first. It was introduced by **Alex F. Osborn**, an American advertising executive, who described it in his book *Applied Imagination*. It encourages creative thinking and participation. It is not a technique for memorising, testing or revising content.
Osborn's rules for the idea-generation stage are:
1. **Defer judgement.** No criticism or evaluation while ideas are being produced.
2. **Aim at quantity.** The more ideas, the more likely a good one is among them.
3. **Welcome unusual ideas.** Wild or odd ideas are encouraged.
4. **Combine and build on others' ideas.**
Evaluation and the sorting of ideas for quality come in a separate, later stage. So the statements "ideas are evaluated as soon as they are offered" and "quantity is discouraged in favour of quality" are both false.
Do not confuse Osborn with others named in options: Edward de Bono is known for lateral thinking and the six thinking hats; J. P. Guilford for the structure of intellect and divergent thinking; Frank Lyman for Think-Pair-Share.
::sub Buzz sessions and group discussion
In a **buzz session** a large class is divided on the spot into small groups of about four to six, which discuss a question for a few minutes and then report back. The name comes from the hum of many conversations at once. It brings participation into large classes.
In a **group discussion** learners do most of the talking and thinking on a topic while the teacher guides. It is a learner-centred method.
::sub Cooperative learning
In **cooperative learning** (सहकारी अधिगम) students work in small, mixed groups towards a **shared goal**. Each member is responsible both for his or her own learning and for the success of the group. It develops academic and social skills together. Its opposites are individual competition for rank and solitary, silent work.
In Elliot Aronson's **jigsaw** technique, a lesson is divided into parts. Each member of a "home group" receives one part. Members holding the same part meet as an "expert group" to master it, then return to their home groups and teach it. Every member depends on the others for the whole lesson; this is called positive interdependence.
::sub Team teaching
In **team teaching** (दल शिक्षण) two or more teachers pool their strengths and share responsibility for **planning, teaching and evaluating** the same group of students. Each topic can be taught by the teacher best qualified for it. A common pattern combines large-group lectures, small-group discussion with different teachers, and independent study, with all the teachers setting and marking the test together.
Team teaching makes use of each teacher's special competence and requires joint planning. It demands far more coordination than individual teaching, which is its main practical difficulty. It is different from a tutorial system, in which tutors only supplement one teacher's lectures, and from peer tutoring, in which students teach students.
::box example
In the M.A. English department of a university in Rohtak, a unit on Partition literature is taught by three teachers as a team: one lectures to all 120 students on the history, another leads small groups on the novels, and the third on the poetry; together they set the unit test. In week two, two students present seminar papers, each followed by a discussant's comments and open discussion. Week three ends with a symposium in which four invited scholars speak in turn on memory, gender, language and migration, and the audience questions them. To start a project, the class brainstorms possible topics for twenty minutes, writing every idea on the board before any is judged.
::endbox
::box confused
- **Seminar and symposium.** Seminar: one paper discussed in depth. Symposium: several speakers, each on a different aspect, in turn.
- **Symposium and panel.** Symposium speakers present prepared talks without conversing; panel members converse informally under a moderator.
- **Seminar and workshop.** Seminar: paper and discussion. Workshop: practical work and a product.
- **Seminar and conference.** Conference is larger and broader, with plenary and parallel sessions; both include papers.
- **Chairperson, moderator, rapporteur, convener.** Chairperson conducts a seminar or symposium; moderator conducts a panel; rapporteur records and reports; convener organises.
- **Tutorial and lecture.** Tutorial: small group, remedial, supplements; lecture: large group, presentation.
- **Team teaching and peer tutoring.** Team teaching: several teachers share one course; peer tutoring: students teach students.
- **Brainstorming and discussion.** Brainstorming defers all judgement while ideas are produced; ordinary discussion evaluates as it goes.
::endbox
::further
::sub Kinds of tutorial
Tutorials are often classified into three kinds:
- **Group tutorial:** a tutor meets a small group to clear common difficulties after lectures.
- **Supervision tutorial:** a tutor works with one or two students on their own written work, usually an essay, as in the tradition of Oxford and Cambridge.
- **Practical tutorial:** skills are practised in a laboratory or workshop under a tutor.
::sub Forum and fishbowl
A **forum** is an open discussion in which the whole audience may ask questions and make comments from the floor, under a chairperson, usually after a lecture, film, panel or symposium. In a panel or symposium the main talking is done by a few people on the platform; in a forum the audience talks.
In a **fishbowl**, a small inner circle of students discusses an issue while the rest sit in an outer circle and observe both the content and the process. Observers comment afterwards, and the two circles may change places. A buzz group, in contrast, is a brief discussion in many small groups at once; in a round robin each member of a group speaks in turn.
::sub Brainwriting
**Brainwriting** is a variant of brainstorming in which ideas are first written silently, on cards or sheets, before any talk. Quiet members then contribute as much as vocal ones, and nobody waits for a turn. Deferred judgement is kept. The written ideas are then pooled, grouped and discussed.
::sub More cooperative learning techniques
- **Think-Pair-Share**, devised by **Frank Lyman**: each student thinks alone about a question, then discusses it with a partner, then pairs share with the class. It gives every student a chance to take part, even in a large class.
- **STAD (Student Teams-Achievement Divisions)**, developed by **Robert Slavin**: the teacher presents the lesson; students study in mixed teams; each takes an individual quiz; team scores are based on **improvement points**, that is, how far each member improves on his or her own past average. Weak students can contribute as much as strong ones.
- **Group Investigation**, associated with Herbert Thelen and developed by Shlomo and Yael Sharan: groups choose sub-topics, plan and carry out an inquiry, and present their findings.
- **Jigsaw** (Aronson), described in the Core.
::sub What makes cooperation work
David W. Johnson and Roger T. Johnson list five essentials of cooperative learning:
1. **Positive interdependence:** each member succeeds only if the others succeed.
2. **Individual accountability:** each member's contribution and learning is assessed, so nobody can leave the work to others (the problem called free riding).
3. **Face-to-face (promotive) interaction:** members help and encourage one another directly.
4. **Social skills:** leadership, communication, trust and handling conflict are taught and used.
5. **Group processing:** the group reflects on how well it is working and how to improve.
The Johnsons also distinguish three goal structures:
- **Positive interdependence (cooperation):** I reach my goal only if the others reach theirs.
- **Negative interdependence (competition):** I reach my goal only if others fail to reach theirs. A rule that only the top five may get grade A creates negative interdependence.
- **No interdependence (individualistic work):** my result is unaffected by others.
From his review of research, Robert Slavin concluded that group work raises achievement most reliably when two conditions are present: **group goals** (or group rewards) and **individual accountability**, so that the team's success depends on the learning of every member. If only the group product is assessed, some members do the work and others ride free.
::sub The Trump Plan
Team teaching is associated with **J. Lloyd Trump**, who proposed reorganising the timetable roughly as follows: about 40 per cent of students' time in **large-group instruction** by the most competent teacher, about 20 per cent in **small-group discussion**, and about 40 per cent in **independent study**. Teachers work as a team and are deployed according to their strengths. The two largest shares, therefore, go to large-group instruction and independent study.

::section 4.4 | Learner-centred and activity methods
::syllabus Methods of teaching in Institutions of higher learning: Teacher centred vs. Learner centred methods
::box footprint
{{FP 4.4}}
::endbox
::h Core
The methods in this section put the learner to work: finding out, solving, making, deciding, acting. Most questions ask who devised a method, what its steps are in order, or how two similar methods differ.
::sub The project method
The **project method** (परियोजना विधि) was set out by **William Heard Kilpatrick** in an essay of 1918. Kilpatrick was a follower of **John Dewey**, and the method rests on Dewey's pragmatism and his idea of **learning by doing**. Kilpatrick defined a project as "a whole-hearted purposeful activity proceeding in a social environment".
In the project method students carry out a purposeful, real-life task from start to finish: they choose it, plan it, do it and judge the result. It replaces rote learning and passive listening with the learners' own activity. A project normally ends in a tangible product: a report, a survey, a model, an exhibition.
Kilpatrick's four steps, in order, are:
1. **Purposing:** the students choose a project that has a real purpose for them.
2. **Planning:** they decide how to carry it out.
3. **Executing:** they do the work.
4. **Evaluating** (judging): they review the result against the purpose with which they started.
Some Indian textbooks add "providing a situation" before purposing and "recording" after evaluating, making six steps. The four central steps and their order stay the same.
A project draws on several subjects at once. A project on village sanitation, for example, uses statistics, sociology, health science and report writing together. So the project method **promotes correlation** among subjects. This is possible because it does not follow a timetable of separate subject periods.
Its limitations: projects take much time, and the knowledge gained is **incidental** and not in systematic order. So the method is hard to use as the only method for covering a prescribed syllabus. (That Kilpatrick propounded it is true, but it does not explain this difficulty.)
Do not confuse Kilpatrick with others: **Maria Montessori** and **Friedrich Froebel** devised methods for early childhood education, and **Helen Parkhurst** devised the **Dalton Plan** (डाल्टन योजना), in which each pupil works through monthly assignments, or "contracts", at his or her own pace in subject rooms called laboratories.
::sub The problem-solving method
In the **problem-solving method** (समस्या समाधान विधि) learners meet a real problem and work towards a solution by the steps of scientific inquiry:
1. Recognising and defining the problem.
2. Collecting relevant data.
3. Forming hypotheses (possible solutions).
4. Testing the hypotheses.
5. Drawing a conclusion.
The first step is always to identify the problem: nothing can be collected or tested before that. The method develops reasoning and a scientific attitude; it makes little use of rote memory or mechanical drill.
The method is based on John Dewey's analysis of reflective thinking in his book *How We Think*. Dewey's five steps are:
1. A felt difficulty.
2. Its location and definition.
3. Suggestion of possible solutions.
4. Reasoning out the bearings (implications) of each suggestion.
5. Further observation or experiment, leading to acceptance or rejection of the suggestion.
**Problem-solving and project compared.** Both involve purposeful activity by learners. But problem-solving is chiefly mental and ends in a solution or conclusion; a project is carried out in a real-life setting and ends in a tangible product. They are not the same in every respect.
::sub Problem-based learning
**Problem-based learning** (PBL) (समस्या आधारित अधिगम) is a modern form in which small groups of students learn through working on complex, real problems. Its marks are:
- the problem comes **first**, before the relevant content is taught, and drives the learning;
- the problems are **ill-structured**: open, with incomplete information and more than one possible answer;
- the tutor acts as a **facilitator** of the group's process, not as a lecturer.
A physical product is not required; that is a mark of the project method.
::sub The heuristic method
The **heuristic method** (अन्वेषण विधि) was advocated by **H. E. Armstrong** (Henry Edward Armstrong), a professor of chemistry in England, for the teaching of science. The word comes from the Greek for "I find out". The pupil is placed in the position of a **discoverer** and finds facts and principles by his or her own observation and experiment. The teacher sets problems and guides but tells as little as possible. A statement that "in the heuristic method the teacher supplies all the facts and conclusions" is false: that is the very practice the method opposes.
::sub Inductive, deductive and heuristic methods
::table widths=22,40,38
|Method|Direction|When it suits|
|Inductive|From particular examples to a general rule|Discovery and understanding; slower, because many examples are needed|
|Deductive|From a general rule to particular cases|Application, practice and revision of an established rule; quicker|
|Heuristic|The learner finds out by own inquiry|Developing scientific attitude; slow|
|Analytic|From the unknown (what is to be proved) back to the known|Discovering how to prove or solve|
|Synthetic|From the known to the unknown|Setting out a proof neatly|
If a teacher states the formula for the area of a triangle and then has students solve problems with it, the method is deductive. If students measure many triangles and arrive at the formula themselves, it is inductive. Induction takes more time; deduction is suitable for practice and revision.
There is also a point of logic. Induction goes from a limited number of instances to a general rule, so its conclusion claims more than the evidence contains and is only **probable**: a new case may always contradict it. Deduction applies a general premise to a particular case; if the premises are true and the form is valid, the conclusion is **certain**. "All metals expand on heating; copper is a metal; therefore copper expands on heating" is deduction. Two other forms appear as distractors: **abduction** infers the best explanation of what is observed, and **analogy** argues from resemblance.
::sub The case method
In the **case method** (व्यष्टि अध्ययन विधि) students study a **case**: a written account of a real or realistic problem situation. They study the facts, identify the issues, discuss alternatives and defend a decision. The method links theory with practice and develops analysis, judgement and decision-making.
The case method was developed at **Harvard**: first in the Law School, and then, from the 1920s, at **Harvard Business School**, which made it famous for management education. It is now used in management, law, medicine and teacher education.
**Case or project?** If students analyse a case already written up for them, it is the case method. If they go out, collect data and produce a report on a real task, it is a project.
::sub Role play
In **role play** (भूमिका निर्वाह) learners take the parts of people in a real or imagined situation and act it out **without a script**. Players are given a situation and a role and improvise. A memorised script would turn it into a drama (dramatisation).
By acting as another person, a learner experiences that person's situation and feelings. Role play is therefore used for empathy, attitudes, communication and interpersonal skills, which belong to the affective domain, not for recall or calculation.
The enactment only provides experience. Learning comes in the **debriefing**: players and observers discuss what happened, how the characters felt and what else could have been done, and link this to the objectives of the lesson. Debriefing is the most important step.
::sub Simulation
In **simulation** (अनुरूपण) learners practise a task in an artificial situation made to resemble the real one: a mock court (moot court) for law students, a flight simulator for pilots, a mock parliament, or simulated teaching among fellow trainees. Mistakes cost nothing, so learners can try, fail and get feedback before facing the real thing. Simulation is used above all for skills, decisions and attitudes that are costly or risky to practise in reality, such as flying, surgery or classroom teaching. Like role play, it must be followed by **debriefing**, where the experience is analysed and turned into learning. Working in a real workplace under a supervisor is an internship or apprenticeship, not a simulation.
::sub Mastery learning
**Mastery learning** (निपुणता अधिगम) was developed by **Benjamin S. Bloom**. Its idea is that nearly all learners can master what is taught if they are given enough time and the right help. The course is divided into units, and each unit has a fixed standard of mastery, for example 80 per cent, that every learner is expected to reach before moving on.
After each unit, a short **formative test** shows each learner which objectives have and have not been mastered. These tests are not graded. Learners who fall short receive **correctives** (re-teaching, different materials, peer help) and then take a second, parallel test. Grades are given only by a later **summative test** (योगात्मक मूल्यांकन). Because performance is judged against a fixed standard, not against other learners, mastery learning relies on **criterion-referenced tests**, not norm-referenced ranking.
::sub The Personalised System of Instruction (Keller Plan)
The **Personalised System of Instruction** (PSI), also called the **Keller Plan** (वैयक्तिकृत अनुदेशन प्रणाली), was developed by the American psychologist **Fred S. Keller** and colleagues in the 1960s and described in his paper "Good-bye, Teacher..." (1968). It applies behavioural principles to a whole university course. Its features are:
1. **Self-pacing:** each student moves through the course at his or her own speed.
2. **Unit mastery:** the course is divided into units; a student must pass a test on one unit before going to the next. A student who fails simply studies again and retakes the test, with no penalty.
3. **Proctors:** the unit tests are marked at once, and discussed with the student, by **proctors**, usually advanced students who have already done the course. This gives immediate feedback and a personal, social element.
4. **Written materials:** the main content is in written study guides and readings, not in lectures.
5. **Lectures for motivation only:** the few lectures and demonstrations are meant to inspire, not to convey essential content, and attendance is optional.
Keep the names apart: Skinner is linked with linear programming, Crowder with branching programming, Bloom with mastery learning, and Keller with PSI. PSI and mastery learning share the idea of unit mastery, but in PSI each student goes at his or her own pace, while in Bloom's mastery learning the class usually moves together with group teaching, and correctives are given to those who need them.
::box example
In a B.Ed. college in Bhiwani, trainees run a project: they survey the number of out-of-school girls in two villages (purposing and planning with the teacher), collect the data (executing), present a report to the gram sabha and judge it against their aim (evaluating). In a management class at a university in Gurugram, students analyse a written case on a cooperative milk plant and defend a decision. In a law college, students argue a moot court, a simulation. In a sociology class, students role-play a meeting between a family and a panchayat member about a dowry dispute, and then spend twenty minutes in debriefing.
::endbox
::box confused
- **Project and case method.** Project: students carry out a real task in the field and produce something. Case: students analyse a situation already written up.
- **Project and problem-solving.** Both purposeful. Problem-solving is mainly mental and ends in a solution; a project is a real-life activity ending in a product.
- **Project method and PBL.** PBL presents an ill-structured problem first, with a facilitator; a product is not required. A product is the mark of the project method.
- **Role play and simulation.** Role play: acting a person's part, chiefly for empathy and attitudes. Simulation: practising a task in a situation built to resemble reality, chiefly for skills and decisions. Both need debriefing.
- **Role play and dramatisation.** Role play is unscripted; dramatisation uses a learnt script.
- **Heuristic and inductive.** Both move towards discovery, but heuristic means the learner finds things out by own experiment with minimal telling; inductive names the direction of reasoning, from examples to rule.
- **Kilpatrick, Parkhurst, Armstrong, Montessori.** Project method; Dalton Plan; heuristic method; early childhood method.
- **Mastery learning and PSI.** Bloom: class taught together, formative tests and correctives. Keller: each student self-paced through written units with proctors.
- **Criterion-referenced and norm-referenced.** Mastery is judged against a fixed standard (criterion), not against other learners (norm).
::endbox
::further
::sub Three definitions of a project
- **Kilpatrick:** "a whole-hearted purposeful activity proceeding in a social environment".
- **J. A. Stevenson:** "a problematic act carried to completion in its natural setting". Note the three ideas: a problem, completion, a natural setting.
- **P. B. Ballard:** "a bit of real life that has been imported into the school".
::sub Kilpatrick's four types of project
1. **Producer (constructive) project:** making something, such as a model, a garden or a magazine.
2. **Consumer (aesthetic) project:** enjoying and appreciating something, such as a poem, a piece of music or a painting.
3. **Problem project:** solving an intellectual question.
4. **Drill (specific learning) project:** mastering a skill or a body of knowledge.
::sub Polya's four steps
The mathematician George Polya, in *How to Solve It*, gave four steps of problem solving: **understand the problem**, **devise a plan**, **carry out the plan**, and **look back** (check the result and reflect on the method). Looking back is where the solver learns something to use in future problems.
::sub Where problem-based learning began
Problem-based learning was introduced in the medical school of **McMaster University**, Canada, in the late 1960s, by **Howard Barrows** and colleagues. Students first met a patient problem and then learnt the science they needed in order to deal with it. Keep apart: the case method with Harvard, PBL with McMaster, and micro-teaching with Stanford.
::sub Variants of the case method
- In the **incident method**, devised by **Paul Pigors**, the group receives only a short statement of an incident and must dig out the remaining facts by questioning the teacher, then define the issue and decide. It trains fact-finding as well as analysis. In an ordinary case study the facts are supplied in full.
- In the **in-basket** (in-tray) exercise, a simulation, each trainee is given a pile of letters, memos and requests such as might lie on a manager's or principal's desk, and must deal with them under time pressure. The decisions are discussed afterwards.
::sub Psychodrama and sociodrama
**J. L. Moreno** (Jacob Levy Moreno), the founder of psychodrama, distinguished two forms of enactment. **Psychodrama** deals with the private problems of an individual and is used in therapy. **Sociodrama** deals with problems shared by a group or community, such as caste prejudice or dowry. Classroom role play on social issues is sociodrama. In **role reversal** two persons exchange roles, so that each sees the situation from the other's side.
::sub Mathetics and PSI side by side
In a matching item on individualised instruction, remember four lines: Skinner's linear programming, every learner passes through the same small steps; Crowder's branching programming, the learner's response decides the next frame; Gilbert's mathetics, the final step is mastered first and the learner works backwards; Keller's PSI, self-paced units, each passed with a proctor. (Programmed instruction and mathetics are explained in section 4.2.)
::sub Action research
A small project carried out to improve one's own practice often follows the cycle of **action research**, associated with **Kurt Lewin**: **plan, act, observe, reflect**, and then plan again, in a spiral. Its aim is to improve practice in one's own setting, not to produce general laws. Ex post facto research, by contrast, studies effects after the event without any intervention; an experiment manipulates a variable with a control group and aims at generalisation. Action research is taught more fully under Research Aptitude (Unit II).

::section 4.5 | Off-line and on-line teaching; blended learning and the flipped classroom
::syllabus Methods of teaching in Institutions of higher learning: Off-line vs. On-line methods
::box footprint
{{FP 4.5}}
::endbox
::h Core
::sub Off-line and on-line teaching
**Off-line teaching** (ऑफ़लाइन शिक्षण) is the ordinary face-to-face teaching of a classroom, laboratory or tutorial, where teacher and learners are physically together. **On-line teaching** (ऑनलाइन शिक्षण) uses the internet to connect teacher and learners who may be far apart. On-line teaching can be synchronous or asynchronous, and it can be mixed with face-to-face teaching in blended learning.
::table widths=26,37,37
|Point|Off-line (face-to-face)|On-line|
|Place|Same room|Anywhere with a connection|
|Time|Fixed timetable|Live (synchronous) or at the learner's convenience (asynchronous)|
|Interaction|Immediate, with gestures and social presence|Through video, chat, forums, e-mail|
|Practical work|Direct, in laboratory or field|Limited; virtual labs and simulations|
|Needs|Campus, teachers, timetable|Devices, internet, digital skills, self-discipline|
|Strength|Social presence, immediate feedback, practical work|Flexibility of time, place and pace; reach to large numbers|
::sub Synchronous and asynchronous learning
**Synchronous learning** (समकालिक अधिगम) happens at the same time for everyone: teacher and learners are online together and interact in real time. Examples: a live video class, a webinar, instant chat.
**Asynchronous learning** (असमकालिक अधिगम) does not need everyone at the same time. Each learner takes part at a convenient time. Examples: recorded lectures, discussion boards or forums, e-mail, assignments, and a quiz that stays open over a day or a week. Asynchronous learning gives time for reflection before replying.
The test is simple: must everyone be present at the same moment? If yes, synchronous; if no, asynchronous. A quiz that may be taken at any time on Saturday is asynchronous, because learners take it at different times.
::table widths=50,50
|Synchronous (same time)|Asynchronous (different times)|
|Live video class|Recorded lecture|
|Webinar|Discussion forum or board|
|Instant chat|E-mail|
|Live phone or video tutorial|Assignment or quiz open over a period|
A **threaded discussion forum** comes closest to a seminar without requiring everyone at once: a paper or prompt is posted, and participants reply to it and to one another over several days. A webinar allows discussion only live. A podcast and a broadcast e-mail are one-way.
::sub Blended learning
**Blended learning** (मिश्रित अधिगम), also called **hybrid learning**, is a planned mix of face-to-face classroom teaching and online learning within one course, so that each mode does what it does best. It keeps the social presence, immediate feedback and practical work of the classroom and adds the flexibility, variety and self-pacing of online study. Because online activities can be done at any hour, outside the timetable, blended learning also extends learning time beyond the classroom.
Blended learning still needs a campus, devices and, above all, careful planning of what is done in each mode. It does not remove the classroom, and it does not reduce planning.
Distinguish it from three other terms that appear as options:
- **Distance learning** separates teacher and learner for most of the course.
- **Programmed learning** uses self-instructional frames (section 4.2).
- **Mastery learning** requires each unit to be mastered before the next (section 4.4).
::sub The flipped classroom
In the **flipped classroom** (फ़्लिप्ड कक्षा) the usual order is reversed. Students meet new content **before class**, at home, usually through short recorded videos or readings. **Class time** is then used for discussion, clearing doubts, problem-solving and application, with the teacher and classmates at hand. The lecture has not disappeared; it has moved out of the classroom in recorded form.
In terms of Bloom's taxonomy, the flipped classroom sends the lower-order work (remembering and understanding, the first exposure) to home, and keeps the higher-order work (applying, analysing, evaluating and creating) for class, where help is available. That is why a flipped classroom frees class time for higher-order activities. A statement that "in a flipped classroom new content is first presented through the teacher's lecture in class" is false: it describes the conventional classroom.
**Flipped and blended.** Blended learning is the general idea of combining face-to-face and online learning. The flipped classroom is one model of blended learning. So every flipped classroom is blended, but not every blended course is flipped. Other blended arrangements, such as station rotation or the flex model, are not flipped.
::sub MOOC types in brief
Two kinds of MOOC (massive open online course) are often named in questions on online learning. **cMOOCs** are connectivist: learners build and share knowledge across networks. **xMOOCs** are structured, instructor-led courses of video lectures and quizzes on large platforms. Section 4.6 explains both.
::box example
In a B.Com. class of 70 at a college in Fatehabad, the teacher uploads a twelve-minute video on depreciation methods and a short reading on the college's learning portal on Sunday. Students watch it when convenient (asynchronous) and post questions on the course forum. In class on Tuesday, after a three-question entry quiz, groups solve depreciation problems on real company accounts while the teacher moves among them (flipped classroom). On Thursday evening she holds a live online doubt-clearing session (synchronous). The whole course, mixing class and online work, is blended learning.
::endbox
::box confused
- **Synchronous and asynchronous.** Same time (live class, webinar, chat) against different times (recorded lecture, forum, e-mail).
- **Blended and flipped.** Blended is the general mix of face-to-face and online; flipped is one blended model in which first exposure to content is online and class is for application.
- **Blended and distance learning.** Blended keeps regular face-to-face classes; distance learning separates teacher and learner for most of the course.
- **Flipped and conventional classroom.** Flipped: content at home, application in class. Conventional: content in class (lecture), application at home (homework).
- **Hybrid and blended.** Two names for the same thing.
::endbox
::further
::sub Where the flipped classroom came from
The flipped classroom was popularised by **Jonathan Bergmann and Aaron Sams**, two chemistry teachers at a high school in Colorado, United States, who began recording their lectures for students in 2007 and later described the approach in their book *Flip Your Classroom*. Keep apart the pairs often offered as distractors: Hase and Kenyon (heutagogy), Siemens and Downes (connectivism), Joyce and Weil (models of teaching).
::sub When students come unprepared
A common difficulty in a flipped course is that some students arrive without having watched the video. If the teacher re-lectures the whole class each time, the incentive to prepare disappears and the class time is lost. The response most consistent with the model is to use a brief entry quiz or task that holds students accountable, and to go ahead with the planned activity, while the unprepared catch up.
::sub Models of blended learning
The Clayton Christensen Institute describes four main models of blended learning:
- **Rotation model:** students rotate, on a fixed schedule or at the teacher's direction, between online learning and other modes such as small-group teaching or pen-and-paper work. Station rotation, lab rotation, individual rotation and the flipped classroom are sub-types of the rotation model.
- **Flex model:** online learning is the backbone of the course, and teachers give face-to-face support flexibly as needed.
- **A la carte (self-blend) model:** a student takes one or more courses entirely online, in addition to the usual courses on campus.
- **Enriched virtual model:** students do most of their work remotely online and attend required face-to-face sessions from time to time.
::sub Transactional distance
In distance education (दूरस्थ शिक्षा), **Michael G. Moore** proposed the theory of **transactional distance**. The distance that matters, he argued, is psychological and communicative, not geographical. It depends on three variables: **dialogue** (interaction between teacher and learner), **structure** (how rigidly the course is laid out) and **learner autonomy**. Transactional distance is greatest when structure is high and dialogue is low; such a course demands the most autonomy from the learner. More dialogue reduces the distance.

::section 4.6 | MOOCs, SWAYAM, SWAYAM PRABHA and other national digital initiatives
::syllabus Off-line vs. On-line methods (Swayam, Swayamprabha, MOOCs etc.)
::box footprint
{{FP 4.6}}
::endbox
::h Core
This section is mostly facts: names, full forms, who runs what, and a few figures. Learn the full forms exactly, and keep the platforms apart by what each one does. Figures that change are collected in the Fact watch box.
::sub What a MOOC is
A **MOOC** (massive open online course) (विशाल मुक्त ऑनलाइन पाठ्यक्रम) is a course offered through the internet to anyone who wishes to join. Take the name word by word:
- **Massive:** there is no fixed limit on enrolment; thousands of learners anywhere can join the same course. It does not mean a long course, a large fee or large video files.
- **Open:** anyone may enrol, without entry qualification and without being a student of a particular institution or place. Study is usually free; a fee is normally charged only for a certificate.
- **Online:** the course is delivered through the internet.
- **Course:** it is a real course, with instructors, a schedule (usually a few weeks), study material and assessments. "Open" does not mean that there are no instructors, no deadlines or no tests.
MOOCs are video-centred: short recorded lectures are the core, with readings, quizzes and forums around them.
Their best-known weakness is a **low completion rate**. Because enrolment is open and free, very large numbers register, many out of curiosity, and only a small proportion finish. Completion rates are commonly reported in single figures or low double figures (in per cent). Lack of personal contact, the self-discipline needed, and weak incentives are the usual reasons. Openness itself is one cause of low completion.
::sub SWAYAM
**SWAYAM** stands for **Study Webs of Active-Learning for Young Aspiring Minds**. It is India's national platform for MOOCs, set up by the **Ministry of Education** (then called the Ministry of Human Resource Development, MHRD). It was launched on 9 July 2017 by the President of India at a National Convention on Digital Initiatives, together with the SWAYAM PRABHA channels and the National Academic Depository.
The main facts about SWAYAM:
- It is designed around three principles of education policy: **access, equity and quality**.
- Courses range from school level through undergraduate and postgraduate levels to skill and teacher-training courses.
- Anyone may enrol, of any age, anywhere. There is no age bar, and it is not restricted to IIT students or to any group.
- Learning is **free**. A learner who wants a certificate registers for a **proctored examination** (a supervised examination held at a centre, पर्यवेक्षित परीक्षा), for which a fee is charged, and must pass it.
- Credits earned can be transferred (credit transfer, क्रेडिट अंतरण) to the learner's own university under UGC regulations (see below).
- SWAYAM is one of the components of the Ministry of Education's **PM e-Vidya** programme for multi-mode access to digital education, along with DIKSHA, SWAYAM PRABHA, radio and podcasts.
::sub The four quadrants of a SWAYAM course
Every SWAYAM course is built in four parts, called the **four quadrants**:
1. **e-Tutorial:** video lectures, with animations, simulations and demonstrations where useful.
2. **e-Content:** reading material that can be downloaded or printed: notes, e-books, case studies.
3. **Discussion forum:** an online forum where learners raise doubts and the course team and other learners answer them.
4. **Assessment (self-assessment):** quizzes, assignments and tests.
Printed correspondence lessons sent by post, residential workshops, weekly face-to-face contact classes and compulsory laboratory attendance are not quadrants; they belong to older distance education or to campus teaching. Books differ in the order in which they number the third and fourth quadrants; learn the four parts, not the numbers.
::sub National Coordinators of SWAYAM
SWAYAM courses are prepared through **National Coordinators** (राष्ट्रीय समन्वयक), each responsible for one sector. The Ministry of Education now lists ten:

|CEC (Consortium for Educational Communication)|Non-technical undergraduate education|
|AICTE (All India Council for Technical Education)|Self-paced and international courses|
|IGNOU (Indira Gandhi National Open University)|Out-of-school students; diploma and certificate courses|
|IIM Bangalore (IIMB)|Management studies|

For engineering, the answer is NPTEL; for undergraduate non-technical courses, CEC; for postgraduate non-technical courses, UGC.
::sub SWAYAM PRABHA
**SWAYAM PRABHA** is a group of **DTH (direct-to-home) television channels** that telecast curriculum-based educational programmes **24 hours a day, 7 days a week**. The signal is carried by the GSAT-15 satellite and uplinked from BISAG-N, Gandhinagar. The channels are free on **DD Free Dish**. New content of at least four hours is shown every day and repeated five more times, so learners can choose a convenient time. Content comes from NPTEL, the IITs, UGC, CEC, IGNOU, NCERT and NIOS, among others.
SWAYAM PRABHA exists for learners who have a television and a dish but **no internet**. It needs no internet connection at the learner's end.
In terms of communication, a television broadcast reaches a large, scattered audience through a mass medium, and viewers cannot reply through the same channel. SWAYAM PRABHA is therefore **one-way mass communication** (एकतरफ़ा जनसंचार). It has no quizzes, no examinations and no credits of its own; interaction comes through its web portal or through SWAYAM courses.

::sub Credit transfer: the UGC regulations
The UGC has made regulations that let a student's own university count SWAYAM courses towards a degree. Under the **UGC (Credit Framework for Online Learning Courses through SWAYAM) Regulations, 2021**, an institution may allow up to **40 per cent** of the total courses offered in a particular programme in a semester to be taken through SWAYAM, and must give credit for them. The earlier Regulations of 2016 had allowed up to 20 per cent. (See the Fact watch box.) Credit transfer is thus a consequence of this regulatory permission.
::sub NPTEL
**NPTEL** (National Programme on Technology Enhanced Learning) was started in **2003** by seven Indian Institutes of Technology (Bombay, Delhi, Kanpur, Kharagpur, Madras, Guwahati and Roorkee) and the Indian Institute of Science, Bangalore, with funding from the Ministry of Education (then MHRD). It offers free online courses, chiefly in engineering and science but also in management and the humanities, with optional proctored examinations for certificates. It is the SWAYAM National Coordinator for engineering. It was not started by NCERT or by the IIMs.

**Fact watch (checked October 2026)**
- **SWAYAM launch:** 9 July 2017, by the President of India, at the National Convention on Digital Initiatives (Ministry of Human Resource Development, now Ministry of Education). The same event launched 32 SWAYAM PRABHA channels and the National Academic Depository.
- **SWAYAM National Coordinators: ten** (NPTEL, UGC, AICTE, CEC, IGNOU, IIMB, NITTTR, INI, NCERT, NIOS), as listed in the Ministry of Education's PIB backgrounder on PM e-Vidya of March 2026, with figures as of January 2026. Older books and older SWAYAM pages list **nine**, without INI; Banaras Hindu University was designated National Coordinator for Institutions of National Importance in 2025.
- **SWAYAM PRABHA channels:** the service began in 2017 with **32** channels; later sources gave 34, and then **40**. In 2026 official sources disagree. The PIB backgrounder on PM e-Vidya of 10 March 2026, and a PIB document of June 2026, describe a group of **48** DTH channels. The SWAYAM PRABHA website and a PIB factsheet of August 2026 give **40**. The documents do not explain the difference. If a question names the March 2026 note, its figure is 48; if a question asks only "how many channels", look for 40 or 48 among the options and check the latest official figure before your examination.
- **Credit transfer limit:** up to **40 per cent** of the total courses offered in a programme in a semester, under the UGC (Credit Framework for Online Learning Courses through SWAYAM) Regulations, 2021, notified in the Gazette of India on 25 March 2021. These replaced the Regulations of 2016 (notified 19 July 2016), which allowed up to **20 per cent**. No later amendment to the 40 per cent ceiling was found; a reply in the Lok Sabha in July 2026 still gave 40 per cent.
- **Four quadrants:** e-Tutorial, e-Content, discussion forum, assessment. Revised SWAYAM Guidelines were approved by the SWAYAM Board in 2024 (circulated in May 2024) and describe a broader course framework; the four quadrants remain the description in Ministry of Education material of 2026 and the answer the examination expects.
- **Certificate fee:** charged for the proctored examination; on NPTEL course pages it is usually Rs 1,000 per course, but the amount is set course by course.
- **SWAYAM Plus:** a separate platform for industry-relevant and skill courses, launched in February 2024 and operated by IIT Madras. Do not confuse it with SWAYAM.
- **ShodhShuddhi:** INFLIBNET's plagiarism-detection service began in 2019 with the software Urkund (later called Ouriginal). That contract ended on 30 September 2023, and the service has used DrillBit since 1 November 2023. It is meant for theses, synopses and research articles, through universities.
- **One Nation One Subscription:** approved by the Union Cabinet on 25 November 2024 and operational from 1 January 2025, with INFLIBNET as implementing agency; about 13,000 e-journals of 30 international publishers for government-funded institutions. It builds on the e-ShodhSindhu consortium (formed in December 2015 by merging UGC-INFONET, N-LIST and INDEST-AICTE). Older books name e-ShodhSindhu as the national journal consortium.
- **NDLI:** pilot from 2015; nationally launched on 19 June 2018.
::endbox

::sub cMOOCs and xMOOCs
The first MOOCs were run by **George Siemens and Stephen Downes** on the principles of **connectivism** (संयोजनवाद), the theory that learning is the forming of connections across a network of people and resources. These are called **cMOOCs**. In a cMOOC much of the content is found, created, remixed and shared by the learners themselves, and the course acts as a hub of a network rather than as a fixed body of content. Because connectivism treats learning as network-building, learners must take responsibility for the content.
**xMOOCs** came later. They are structured, instructor-led courses with a fixed syllabus of video lectures, quizzes and examinations, offered on large platforms such as Coursera, edX and SWAYAM. They are content-delivery courses.
So "xMOOCs are based on connectivism" is false, and "cMOOCs follow a fixed syllabus of instructor-made video lectures" is also false: each statement gives one type the feature of the other.
A **SPOC** (small private online course) uses MOOC-style materials, such as video lectures and automatic quizzes, for a limited, selected group, typically the students of one institution. It allows closer interaction and is often used in blended teaching on campus.
::sub Reading a completion rate
A completion rate is a fraction, and its meaning depends on the base (the denominator). Always say which base is used.
::box worked
A MOOC has 40,000 enrolments. 8,000 learners begin the first week's work. 2,400 complete the course.
> Completion rate on enrolments = completers / enrolments = 2,400 / 40,000 = 0.06 = 6 per cent
> Completion rate on starters = completers / starters = 2,400 / 8,000 = 0.30 = 30 per cent
> Share of enrolled learners who ever started = 8,000 / 40,000 = 0.20 = 20 per cent
The same course can be reported as having a 6 per cent or a 30 per cent completion rate. Neither is wrong; they answer different questions.
::endbox
::box slips
- Dividing by the wrong base: the question says "on enrolments" or "on those who began"; read it twice.
- Forgetting to multiply by 100 when the options are in per cent.
- Dividing the larger number by the smaller (for example 40,000 / 2,400) and getting a figure above 100 per cent.
::endbox
::sub Parent and host institution
The UGC's credit framework uses two terms. The **parent institution** is the one in which the student is enrolled; it incorporates the SWAYAM credits into the student's record. The **host institution** is the one whose faculty member offers the course on SWAYAM. If a student of University X takes a course taught by a professor of Institute Y, X is the parent and Y the host.
::sub National Academic Depository and Academic Bank of Credits
The **National Academic Depository** (NAD), launched on 9 July 2017 with SWAYAM, is a round-the-clock online store of academic awards, such as degrees, diplomas, certificates and mark-sheets, lodged by institutions and boards. Employers and institutions can verify them online. The UGC implements NAD with **DigiLocker** as its single depository. The **Academic Bank of Credits** (ABC) is different: it records the credits a student earns, so that they can be transferred and counted towards a degree (see section 6.7). NDLI is a library, and Shodhganga holds theses.
::sub Vidwan and IRINS
Two more INFLIBNET services:
- **Vidwan:** a national database of profiles of experts, that is, scientists, researchers and faculty members, used for example to find experts for committees.
- **IRINS** (Indian Research Information Network System): a web-based research information management system that institutions use to collect and show their faculty's publications and research activity.
::sub The National Educational Technology Forum
The National Education Policy 2020 proposes an autonomous body, the **National Educational Technology Forum** (NETF), to provide a platform for the free exchange of ideas on the use of technology to enhance learning, assessment, planning and administration. Do not confuse it with the **National Research Foundation**, which is meant to fund and promote research, or with the **National Testing Agency**, which conducts examinations such as UGC NET.

::answers
Q1. (B), because Shodhgangotri holds synopses, ShodhShuddhi gives plagiarism-detection software, Vidwan is the expert database and NDLI is the single-window library search.
Q2. (D), because study on SWAYAM is free; a fee is charged only for the certificate examination. The full form, the proctored examination and the forum quadrant are correct.
Q3. (A), because both are true and the DTH broadcast is exactly why no internet is needed.
Q4. (C), because Statement I describes cMOOCs correctly, but Statement II describes a SPOC; an xMOOC is a structured, instructor-led course open to very large numbers.
Q5. (C), because UGC handles non-technical postgraduate courses. NPTEL handles engineering and CEC non-technical undergraduate courses.
Q6. (C), because 1,000 / 5,000 = 0.20, that is 20 per cent. On enrolments it would be 1,000 / 25,000 = 4 per cent, option (A), which uses the wrong base.
Q7. (D), because the 2021 Regulations allow up to 40 per cent. The 20 per cent ceiling, option (B), belonged to the 2016 Regulations.
Q8. (B), because DIKSHA, led by NCERT, serves school students and teachers. e-PG Pathshala is postgraduate, Shodhganga holds theses and NPTEL is mainly engineering.
