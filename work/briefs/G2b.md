
## 2.6 Emotional characteristics and motivation  (26 booklet questions)

### Core must teach
- S1-Q30 [single] Intrinsic motivation comes from within (interest, curiosity, satisfaction in the task); extrinsic depends on marks, prizes, praise or fear of punishment.
- S2-Q18 [single] Maslow's hierarchy: physiological, safety, love/belonging, esteem, self-actualisation; lower needs must be met first. Herzberg (two-factor), McClelland (achievement need), Rogers (humanistic facilitation) are others.
- S3-Q21 [single] Maslow's top need is self-actualisation (realising full potential), above physiological, safety, belonging and esteem.
- S4-Q15 [single] EI was introduced by Salovey and Mayer and popularised by Goleman's Emotional Intelligence (1995): self-awareness, self-regulation, motivation, empathy, social skills. Gardner's inter/intrapersonal intelligences are a different theory; Thurstone = primary mental abilities; Binet = first IQ test.
- S4-Q25 [scenario] Working for a reward outside the activity (scholarship) is extrinsic motivation; studying because the subject is interesting is intrinsic; external rewards overused can weaken interest.
- S5-Q14 [single] Physiological needs (food, water, sleep, shelter) form Maslow's base, followed by safety, love/belonging, esteem, self-actualisation.
- S6-Q4 [single] McClelland: need for achievement (n-Ach), preference for moderately difficult tasks and feedback; Maslow: hierarchy of needs; Rogers: humanistic/facilitation; Lewin: field theory, leadership styles.
- S12-Q36 [assertion-reason] Self-efficacy (Bandura) is belief in one's capability to succeed; high self-efficacy leads learners to choose harder tasks, try harder and persist longer, whereas self-doubters give up early.
- S14-Q28 [single] Maslow's deficiency needs are physiological, safety, belongingness and esteem (they cease to motivate once met); self-actualisation is a growth (being) need that continues to motivate as it is satisfied.
- S15-Q33 [assertion-reason] The Yerkes-Dodson law describes an inverted U: performance improves with arousal (anxiety) up to an optimum and then declines, and the optimum is lower for difficult tasks. Moderate anxiety can facilitate performance; it does not rise steadily.

### Going Further must teach
- S7-Q9 [scenario] Weiner classifies causes by locus and stability: ability internal-stable, effort internal-unstable, task difficulty external-stable, luck external-unstable.
- S8-Q34 [single] Herzberg's two-factor theory: hygiene factors (salary, security, supervision, conditions) prevent dissatisfaction; motivators (achievement, recognition, responsibility, work itself) produce satisfaction.
- S9-Q40 [single] Deci and Ryan SDT: autonomy, competence, relatedness; Maslow: safety, belonging, esteem; McClelland: achievement, power, affiliation; Alderfer ERG: existence, relatedness, growth.
- S10-Q5 [scenario] Rotter's locus of control: internal (own effort, responsibility, persistence) vs external (luck, others); learned helplessness = belief nothing one does matters.
- S10-Q26 [scenario] Overjustification: rewarding an already enjoyed activity shifts the reason to reward, so interest falls below baseline when reward stops; Hawthorne = being observed; Pygmalion = expectations; Zeigarnik = memory for unfinished tasks.
- S11-Q22 [scenario] Carol Dweck: fixed mindset (entity theory) holds ability is unchangeable, so learners avoid challenge and read failure as low ability; growth mindset (incremental theory) holds ability grows with effort. Praising effort and strategy fosters growth mindset.
- S12-Q42 [match] Maslow: hierarchy of needs. Weiner: attribution theory (causes of success and failure). Atkinson: achievement motivation as motive to succeed (hope of success) vs motive to avoid failure. Bandura: self-efficacy.
- S13-Q8 [scenario] Expectancy-value theory (Atkinson, Eccles, Vroom) holds motivation = expectancy of success × value of goal; if either is near zero motivation is near zero, so a valued but seemingly unattainable goal yields low effort.
- S16-Q15 [match] Alderfer: existence, relatedness, growth (ERG). Herzberg: hygiene factors and motivators. Vroom: valence, instrumentality, expectancy. McClelland: needs for achievement, affiliation, power.
- S16-Q22 [scenario] Learned helplessness (Seligman): after uncontrollable failure, learners believe outcomes do not depend on their actions and stop trying even when success is possible. Test anxiety is fear interfering during tests; field dependence is a cognitive style; identity diffusion is Marcia's status.
- S17-Q20 [single] Bandura's sources of self-efficacy: mastery experiences (strongest), vicarious experiences, verbal persuasion, physiological/emotional states.
- S17-Q41 [scenario] Mastery (learning) goals aim to understand and improve; performance goals aim to demonstrate ability relative to others; work-avoidance aims at minimal effort.
- S18-Q16 [scenario] ARCS: Attention (e.g. puzzling case), Relevance (link to goals), Confidence (graded tasks with early success), Satisfaction (rewards, use of learning).
- S19-Q15 [multi] Informational unexpected praise sustains intrinsic motivation; expected tangible rewards for interesting tasks can undermine it; autonomy support promotes it; extrinsic motivation is useful for uninteresting tasks and is not always harmful.
- S20-Q15 [numerical] Vroom: motivational force = expectancy x instrumentality x valence; terms multiply, so any zero term makes motivation zero.
- S20-Q24 [two-statement] Maslow: lower needs must be reasonably (not completely) satisfied before higher needs emerge. McClelland's acquired-needs theory: achievement, affiliation, power are learned, so achievement motivation can be trained.

## 2.7 Adult learners: andragogy and beyond  (22 booklet questions)

### Core must teach
- S1-Q10 [single] Malcolm Knowles popularised andragogy, the art and science of helping adults learn; adults are self-directed, experienced, ready to learn for life roles, problem-centred, internally motivated. Pedagogy is teaching children; Piaget, Skinner, Bloom are unrelated.
- S2-Q35 [single] Pedagogy, from Greek 'leading the child', is teaching children with teacher-directed learning; andragogy is helping adults learn.
- S3-Q29 [single] Knowles: adults need to know why they learn and want immediate application, so prefer problem-centred learning; subject-centred learning for later use is typical of pedagogy.
- S4-Q33 [single] Knowles' assumptions: adults move from dependence to self-direction, use experience as a resource, are ready to learn for their roles, prefer problem-centred learning, are internally motivated.
- S5-Q24 [single] Knowles: adults bring large, varied experience; methods drawing on it (discussion, case studies, problem-solving) work better than one-way transmission.
- S6-Q15 [single] Knowles: adult motivation is chiefly internal (self-esteem, job satisfaction, quality of life, growth); external pressure, fear, small rewards typify children.
- S7-Q11 [multi] Knowles's assumptions: need to know, self-concept, experience, readiness linked to social roles, problem-/life-centred orientation, internal motivation; subject-centred orientation is pedagogy.
- S8-Q12 [single] Andragogy uses learners' experience as a resource and problem-centred participatory methods (case discussion); dictation, drill and lecture-recall treat learners as dependent receivers.
- S9-Q27 [single] Knowles: pedagogy - dependent learner, subject-centred, external motivation; andragogy - self-directing, problem-centred, internal motivation.
- S10-Q48 [scenario] Knowles's self-directed learning: individuals take initiative in diagnosing needs, setting goals, finding resources, choosing strategies, evaluating outcomes; incidental learning is unplanned; programmed learning follows fixed path.
- S15-Q42 [assertion-reason] Knowles's first andragogical assumption: with maturity self-concept moves from dependence towards self-direction; adults therefore resist being treated as dependent recipients of instruction.
- S16-Q31 [scenario] Knowles' assumptions: self-concept, role of experience, readiness to learn (when social-role tasks demand), orientation to learning (immediate application, problem-centred), motivation (internal), need to know.
- S17-Q28 [assertion-reason] Knowles: readiness to learn arises from developmental tasks of social roles; adults have a large reservoir of experience as a resource. These are separate assumptions, neither causing the other.
- S17-Q37 [two-statement] Knowles saw experience as adults' richest resource, but it can also produce fixed habits, biases and assumptions that resist new learning, requiring unlearning.

### Going Further must teach
- S11-Q49 [match] Self-concept (self-directing) implies involving adults in planning; experience implies discussion and case methods drawing on it; readiness to learn follows social-role demands so timing matters; orientation to learning is problem-centred so content is organised around problems.
- S12-Q12 [scenario] Mezirow's transformative learning begins with a disorienting dilemma, proceeds by critical reflection on assumptions and ends in a changed frame of reference. Kolb's cycle explains learning from experience generally; Knowles's andragogy concerns adult learner assumptions; Lave and Wenger's situated learning concerns communities of practice.
- S13-Q48 [single] A learning contract (recommended by Knowles) is a written agreement drawn up in advance by an adult learner with a facilitator stating objectives, resources, evidence of accomplishment and criteria. A portfolio collects evidence of work already done.
- S14-Q13 [scenario] Knowles first opposed andragogy to pedagogy but later treated them as ends of a continuum; adults who are complete novices may need teacher direction at first, moving to self-direction as competence grows.
- S14-Q48 [scenario] K. Patricia Cross: situational barriers (circumstances such as job, family, cost, transport), institutional barriers (provider schedules, rules, fees), dispositional barriers (attitudes and self-perceptions such as feeling too old).
- S15-Q12 [scenario] Heutagogy (Hase and Kenyon) is self-determined learning in which the learner decides what and how to learn and revises goals; it goes beyond andragogy (self-directed within a teacher-designed course). Peeragogy is peer-produced learning.
- S18-Q1 [multi] Andragogy involves learners in diagnosing needs; heutagogy (Hase and Kenyon) emphasises capability and double-loop learning with the learner determining the path; pedagogy-andragogy-heutagogy form a continuum of increasing autonomy.
- S20-Q35 [match] Knowles: andragogy assumptions about self-directing adults. Mezirow: perspective transformation (transformative learning). Freire: critique of banking education, dialogue. Hase and Kenyon: heutagogy, self-determined learning.

## 2.8 Individual differences and intelligence  (22 booklet questions)

### Core must teach
- S1-Q18 [single] Gardner's Frames of Mind (1983) proposed several relatively independent intelligences (linguistic, logical-mathematical, spatial, musical, bodily-kinaesthetic, interpersonal, intrapersonal). Spearman: g factor; Binet: first intelligence test; Cattell: fluid vs crystallised.
- S1-Q25 [single] Individual differences means learners vary in intelligence, aptitude, interests, motivation, background and learning speed, which is why teachers vary methods and pace.
- S2-Q21 [single] Individual differences arise from the interaction of heredity and environment (family, culture, schooling, health, opportunity); neither alone explains them.
- S2-Q30 [single] Spearman's two-factor theory: general factor g plus specific factor s. Gardner, Sternberg and Guilford rejected intelligence as a single ability.
- S3-Q4 [single] IQ = mental age / chronological age x 100 (Stern's idea, used by Terman); 100 is average; modern tests use deviation IQ.
- S4-Q30 [single] Aptitude is future-oriented potential to learn with training; achievement is what has been learnt; interest is liking for an activity; attitude is a settled tendency to respond favourably or unfavourably.
- S5-Q7 [single] Intra-individual differences: variation among one person's own abilities (strong in language, weak in maths). Inter-individual differences: between persons.
- S5-Q44 [single] Binet with Simon produced the first workable intelligence scale in France in 1905, introducing mental age. Wechsler later built adult/child scales; Spearman = g; Cattell = fluid/crystallised.
- S6-Q13 [single] Sternberg: triarchic (analytical, creative, practical); Spearman: two-factor; Thurstone: group-factor (primary mental abilities); Thomson: sampling theory.
- S7-Q48 [scenario] Interpersonal = understanding others' feelings, motives; intrapersonal = understanding oneself; linguistic = language; naturalistic = nature classification.
- S8-Q10 [scenario] Spatial intelligence: perceiving and mentally transforming the visual world (architects, sculptors, navigators, chess); bodily-kinaesthetic = skilled body use; logical-mathematical = reasoning, numbers; naturalistic = classifying nature.
- S9-Q25 [single] Guilford's SI model: operations (e.g. memory, divergent production), contents (e.g. symbolic, semantic), products (e.g. units, relations); 120 or more abilities; Sternberg = analytical/creative/practical; Cattell = fluid/crystallised.
- S10-Q15 [numerical] IQ = mental age / chronological age x 100; MA 12, CA 10 gives 120; inverting gives about 83; IQ 100 means MA = CA.
- S11-Q2 [multi] Gardner (1983) proposed seven relatively independent intelligences and later added the naturalistic. He criticised schools for favouring linguistic and logical-mathematical intelligence, and his theory rejects describing a learner by a single IQ score.
- S12-Q49 [scenario] Sternberg's triarchic theory: analytical (componential) for academic problems, creative (experiential) for novelty, practical (contextual) for adapting to, shaping and selecting everyday environments ('street smarts'). Crystallised intelligence is Cattell's.
- S13-Q35 [multi] Individual differences exist in physical, mental, emotional and social traits, and are differences of degree (quantitative) and kind (qualitative). Uniform teaching does not eliminate them and often widens gaps.
- S15-Q20 [scenario] Cattell: fluid intelligence is reasoning in novel situations and tends to decline from early adulthood; crystallised intelligence is accumulated knowledge and skills (vocabulary, information) and is maintained or grows with age.
- S15-Q48 [match] Spearman: two-factor theory (g and s). Thurstone: seven primary mental abilities. Guilford: structure-of-intellect (operations, contents, products). Sternberg: triarchic theory (analytical, creative, practical).
- S16-Q23 [scenario] Guilford: convergent production seeks one correct answer; divergent production generates many answers and underlies creativity. Unusual-uses tests measure divergent production, scored for fluency, flexibility, originality.
- S19-Q48 [two-statement] Ratio IQ = MA/CA x 100 fails for adults because mental age levels off in late teens; Wechsler's deviation IQ expresses standing among same-age peers with mean 100, SD 15.

### Going Further must teach
- S14-Q25 [single] The Flynn effect (James Flynn) is the generational rise in average IQ scores, attributed to nutrition, schooling and environment, showing intelligence is not fixed by heredity alone. The Matthew effect is accumulation of early advantage; Pygmalion and halo effects concern expectations and rating bias.
- S18-Q26 [single] PASS (Das, Naglieri and Kirby, after Luria): Planning, Attention-arousal, Simultaneous and Successive processing; basis of the Cognitive Assessment System.

## 2.9 Learners with diverse needs and inclusive teaching  (20 booklet questions)

### Core must teach
- S2-Q5 [single] Gifted learners perform far above their age group and need enrichment and challenge; slow learners need more time, repetition and concrete examples.
- S3-Q24 [single] Slow learners need slower pace, simple language, concrete examples, repetition, frequent encouragement and peer tutoring; speeding up or withholding feedback widens the gap.
- S3-Q40 [single] To meet individual differences teachers vary methods and pace, give graded tasks and extra help or enrichment (differentiated instruction); one fixed method or common task suits few.
- S4-Q18 [single] The Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act, 2016 replaced the PwD Act 1995, raised recognised disabilities from 7 to 21, and requires inclusive education and reasonable accommodation. RTE Act is 2009.
- S5-Q37 [single] Braille is a raised-dot system read by touch for blind/low-vision learners; hearing-impaired learners use sign language and captions.
- S6-Q18 [single] Inclusion: learners of all abilities and backgrounds, including those with disabilities, learn together with needed supports; required under RPwD Act 2016; not segregation or one method for all.
- S6-Q39 [single] Dyslexia = reading/spelling difficulty; dyscalculia = calculation; dysgraphia = handwriting; dyspraxia = movement coordination.
- S7-Q25 [scenario] Provisions for gifted: enrichment, acceleration, independent projects, mentoring; repetition increases boredom; exemption wastes ability.
- S8-Q14 [single] Individual differences are met by flexible grouping and graded/tiered assignments; uniform lecture, pace and homework ignore readiness differences; public ranking harms.
- S9-Q23 [scenario] Hearing impairment: visual channels - captions, written outlines, visual aids, front seat for lip-reading, sign language; Braille/large print serve visual impairment.
- S12-Q6 [multi] Gifted learners need enrichment or acceleration; slow learners have somewhat below-average general ability; specific learning disabilities (e.g. dyslexia) occur despite average or above-average intelligence; underachievers perform below measured potential.
- S13-Q4 [single] Acceleration moves gifted learners faster through the curriculum (grade skipping, early entry, advanced courses); enrichment adds depth and breadth at the same grade; ability grouping places similar-ability learners together; remediation corrects deficiencies.
- S15-Q6 [scenario] An underachiever has adequate or high measured ability but attains far below it, usually for motivational, emotional or environmental reasons. A slow learner has below-average ability and attains accordingly; dyscalculia is a specific learning disability in mathematics.
- S16-Q44 [scenario] Integration places the learner in the regular setting without changing the system; inclusion requires the institution to change, removing barriers and providing accommodation. Segregation means separate settings; exclusion means denial of education.
- S17-Q32 [scenario] Dysgraphia = writing difficulty (letter formation, spacing); dyslexia = reading; dyscalculia = number; dyspraxia = planning and coordination of movement.
- S19-Q4 [single] RPwD Act 2016 requires government and government-aided higher education institutions to reserve not less than 5 per cent seats for persons with benchmark disabilities, with 5 years upper age relaxation; 4 per cent applies to government employment.
- S19-Q38 [single] Alternative text on images is read by screen readers for learners with visual impairment; captions on videos serve learners with hearing impairment.

### Going Further must teach
- S6-Q43 [single] Differentiated instruction (Carol Ann Tomlinson): varying content, process and product by readiness, interest, learning profile; direct, programmed and mass instruction do not.
- S10-Q29 [single] UDL (CAST) plans variety from the start - multiple means of representation, engagement and action/expression - benefiting all and reducing later accommodation; not segregation or uniform method.
- S11-Q7 [scenario] Tomlinson's differentiated instruction varies content (what is learnt), process (how learners make sense of it), product (how they show learning) and learning environment. Letting students show learning by essay, concept map or presentation differentiates the product.

## Booklet 0 (diagnostic) questions the guide must also teach
- P1-Q14 -> 2.7: answer and explanation: (B)**, because andragogy, as set out by Malcolm Knowles, describes adult learners as self-directed (1), as bringing rich experience to learning (3) and as problem-centred rather than subject-centred (4). Statement 2 is wrong because adults want a say in what they learn, and statement 5 is wrong because their motivation is chiefly internal, not driven by external rewards. 
- P4-Q6 -> 2.8: answer and explanation: (C)**, because interpersonal intelligence is the ability to understand the moods, motives and intentions of other people and to work well with them; good teachers and counsellors show it. Intrapersonal intelligence is understanding of oneself, linguistic intelligence is skill with words, and spatial intelligence is the ability to think in images and visualise forms.
