
## 6.6 Continuous and Comprehensive Evaluation and grading  (8 booklet questions)

### Core must teach
- S1-Q1 [single] In CCE, 'continuous' means regular assessment through the year; 'comprehensive' means covering both scholastic (subjects) and co-scholastic areas (life skills, attitudes, values, arts, sports). It is not about written vs oral or internal vs external.
- S4-Q31 [single] Small mark differences are within measurement error; grades place students in broad bands, reducing misclassification, unhealthy competition and stress; examinations are still needed.
- S5-Q4 [single] Grading on a curve fixes in advance the proportion receiving each grade, so grades depend on relative standing (norm-referenced, relative grading); in criterion-referenced (absolute) grading everyone reaching the standard can get top grade.
- S7-Q7 [scenario] Absolute grading fixes boundaries in marks in advance; relative grading (grading on the curve) sets boundaries by group performance, e.g. top 10% get A.
- S10-Q40 [single] CCE scholastic areas: curricular subjects and intellectual abilities; co-scholastic: life skills, attitudes, values, health, physical education, cultural activities.
- S12-Q50 [single] Relative grading distributes grades by position in the group, so in a small, uniformly able class some good performers must receive low grades; absolute grading uses fixed standards and avoids this.
- S16-Q42 [single] Relative grading awards grades by position in the group (norm-referenced); absolute grading uses fixed pre-set standards (criterion-referenced).

### Going Further must teach
- S8-Q46 [single] Direct grading: qualitative judgement straight into grades; indirect grading: marks first then converted by fixed ranges (absolute) or group position (relative).

## 6.7 Evaluation in the Choice Based Credit System  (33 booklet questions)

### Core must teach
- S1-Q38 [single] CBCS = Choice Based Credit System (UGC guidelines 2015): students choose among core, elective and ability enhancement courses; credits, letter grades, grade points, CGPA.  FLAG: DATED: cites UGC CBCS guidelines of 2015; guide should note later CCFUP 2022/NEP frameworks — verify current framing.
- S2-Q32 [single] SGPA = total credit points (credits x grade points) in a semester divided by total credits of that semester; CGPA does the same across all semesters.
- S2-Q43 [single] Under CBCS, core courses are compulsory; electives are chosen from a pool (discipline-specific or generic); ability enhancement courses build general skills; each course carries credits.
- S3-Q20 [single] UGC issued CBCS guidelines in 2015 for a common credit and grading pattern. NCERT handles school education, NCTE teacher education, NAAC accreditation.
- S4-Q23 [single] Under UGC CBCS, one credit generally = one hour lecture/tutorial or two hours practical per week through a semester; credits show relative weight of courses and are used in GPA calculation.  FLAG: DATED: credit-hour equivalence stated per CBCS 2015; UGC CCFUP 2022/NCrF express credits as 15 contact hours (30 for practicals) per semester — guide should present current norm.
- S4-Q48 [single] CGPA is the credit-weighted average of grade points across all courses in all semesters; SGPA is for a single semester; both appear on the transcript.
- S5-Q12 [numerical] Credit points for a course = credits x grade point (e.g., 4 credits x GP 8 = 32); SGPA = sum of credit points / total credits in the semester.
- S6-Q17 [single] SGPA = sum of (course credits x grade points) / sum of course credits for the semester; not divided by number of courses.
- S6-Q26 [single] UGC CBCS: Core, Elective (Discipline Specific Elective from main discipline; Generic Elective from other discipline), Ability Enhancement Courses (AECC and Skill Enhancement Courses).
- S7-Q45 [single] UGC CBCS (2015): Ability Enhancement Compulsory Courses are Environmental Science and English/MIL Communication; Skill Enhancement Courses are skill/value based; core and electives separate.  FLAG: DATED: reflects UGC CBCS 2015 guidelines; CCFUP 2022 renames categories (AEC, SEC, VAC) - guide should present 2015 scheme as the CBCS answer and note the newer framework.
- S8-Q8 [numerical] CGPA = sum(credits x SGPA)/total credits; (20x7 + 30x8)/50 = 380/50 = 7.6; simple average 7.5 is wrong.
- S8-Q17 [single] UGC ten-point scale: O Outstanding 10, A+ Excellent 9, A Very Good 8, B+ Good 7, B Above Average 6, C Average 5, P Pass 4, F Fail 0, Ab Absent 0.
- S9-Q34 [single] UGC scale: O 10, A+ 9, A 8, B+ 7, B 6, C 5, P 4, F 0.
- S10-Q23 [numerical] SGPA = sum(credits x grade point)/total credits: (36+28+10)/10 = 7.4; simple average 7.0 is wrong.
- S10-Q31 [single] CBCS expresses courses in credits on a common pattern so credits earned can be counted at another institution, enabling mobility (now via Academic Bank of Credits).
- S11-Q8 [single] UGC CBCS ten-point scale: O=10, A+=9, A=8, B+=7, B=6, C=5, P=4, F=0 (Ab=0). P (Pass) is the lowest passing grade with 4 points; C (Average) carries 5 and B (Above Average) 6.  FLAG: DATED: grade scale per UGC CBCS guidelines (2015); universities may vary minimum pass grade — verify current UGC/NEP wording
- S13-Q39 [single] UGC CBCS scale: O Outstanding 10, A+ Excellent 9, A Very Good 8, B+ Good 7, B Above Average 6, C Average 5, P Pass 4, F Fail 0.  FLAG: DATED: per UGC CBCS guidelines 2015 — verify still current
- S14-Q2 [numerical] Under UGC CBCS, one credit = one hour of lecture or tutorial, or two hours of practical work, per week over a semester; 3 lecture hours + 2 practical hours = 3 + 1 = 4 credits.
- S15-Q43 [numerical] SGPA = Σ(credits × grade points) / Σ credits; e.g. (54 + 14 + 16) / 10 = 8.4. A simple average of grade points ignores credits and is wrong.
- S16-Q49 [single] UGC CBCS ten-point scale: O 10, A+ 9, A 8, B+ 7, B 6, C 5, P 4, F 0, Ab 0. Credits of F/Ab courses count in SGPA denominator.  FLAG: DOUBT: claim that F/Ab credits still count in SGPA calculation follows the UGC illustration but practice varies by university — verify wording.
- S17-Q40 [scenario] UGC CBCS: Core, Discipline Specific Elective, Generic Elective, Ability Enhancement Compulsory Courses (Environmental Science, English/MIL Communication) and Skill Enhancement Courses (skill-based, chosen from a pool).  FLAG: DATED: uses 2015 UGC CBCS course categories (AECC/SEC/DSE/GE); CCFUP 2022 renamed/reshaped these (AEC, SEC, VAC, MDC) — guide should present both and note which framework the question assumes.
- S19-Q1 [single] Four-year UG under NEP 2020/CCFUP: certificate after 1 year, diploma after 2, bachelor's degree after 3, honours or honours with research after 4; credits held in the Academic Bank of Credits for re-entry.
- S19-Q10 [single] The Academic Bank of Credits stores credits from recognised institutions in a student's account for transfer and redemption toward certificate, diploma or degree, supporting multiple entry and exit; the National Academic Depository stores awards.

### Going Further must teach
- S12-Q33 [numerical] SGPA = Σ(credits × grade points) / Σ credits of all courses taken, including failed courses (F = 0 stays in the denominator). E.g. (0 + 32 + 12) / 10 = 4.4.  FLAG: DOUBT: UGC CBCS formula uses all courses taken; university practice on counting failed courses varies — verify wording in UGC guidelines
- S12-Q43 [single] UGC CBCS guidelines classify courses as core, elective (discipline-specific, generic, dissertation/project) and ability enhancement (AECC, SEC). Dissertation/project is an elective course for special/advanced study done with teacher's advisory support, carrying credits.  FLAG: DATED: classification per UGC CBCS guidelines (2015); CCFUP 2022 uses different course categories — verify which framework the guide presents
- S14-Q37 [numerical] CGPA = total credit points / total credits. Points so far 40 × 6.0 = 240; target 60 × 6.5 = 390; needed 150 over 20 credits = SGPA 7.5. CGPA is credit-weighted, not a simple average of SGPAs.
- S16-Q24 [numerical] CGPA = sum of (semester credits x SGPA) / total credits; not the simple average of SGPAs. Example: (160+140+60)/50 = 7.2.
- S18-Q23 [numerical] Grade points: O 10, A+ 9, A 8, B+ 7, B 6, C 5, P 4. With equal credits SGPA is the mean: (10+9+7+4)/4 = 7.5.
- S18-Q25 [numerical] CGPA = total credit points / total credits: (80x7.0 + 20x8.5)/100 = 730/100 = 7.3; not the simple average of CGPA and SGPA.
- S19-Q36 [single] Averaging requires equal units (interval scale); letter grades are strictly ordinal, so computing SGPA treats grade points as equal steps by convention. Nominal names categories; ratio has a true zero.
- S20-Q6 [numerical] With equal credits SGPA is the mean of grade points; target 7.0 over 4 courses needs 28 points; 28 - 19 = 9 = A+. Ten-point scale: O 10, A+ 9, A 8, B+ 7, B 6, C 5, P 4.
- S20-Q23 [numerical] SGPA = sum(credits x grade points)/total credits, with F counted as 0 over its credits; replacing F (0) with C (5) in a 4-credit course adds 20 credit points over 20 credits = +1.0.  FLAG: DOUBT: assumes the cleared grade replaces F in the same semester's SGPA; universities differ on whether supplementary results revise the original SGPA — verify convention.
- S20-Q26 [single] NHEQF (UGC) levels 4.5-8: 4.5 UG certificate, 5 UG diploma, 5.5 bachelor's, 6 bachelor's honours/research (4-year), 6.5 master's, 7 master's after 4-year UG/1-year, 8 doctorate.  FLAG: DOUBT: explanation places master's at 6.5 only; NHEQF also places some master's (e.g. after 4-year UG / M.Tech) at level 7 — verify level table before teaching.

## 6.8 Computer-based testing  (12 booklet questions)

### Core must teach
- S2-Q24 [single] In a computer-based test questions appear on screen, answers are marked by mouse/keyboard at a centre, and scored electronically; NTA uses this mode for national exams.
- S3-Q30 [single] CBT records responses electronically for near-instant error-free scoring; it still needs test centres, identity verification and reliable equipment; technical failure and unequal computer familiarity are limitations.
- S5-Q6 [single] In developing countries, the digital divide (rural/poor candidates less familiar with computers) and technical failures are CBT's chief limitations; objective scoring, speed and item shuffling are advantages.
- S6-Q29 [single] Mock tests in computer-based testing let candidates practise navigating, marking/changing answers and watching the timer; questions differ from the real exam.
- S7-Q15 [single] CAT estimates ability as it proceeds: correct answer brings harder item, wrong answer easier; precise score with fewer items.
- S8-Q13 [single] In CBT answers can be changed, cleared or marked for review until submission and a question palette shows unanswered items; OMR marks cannot be altered; CBT needs power and does not allow choosing difficulty.
- S9-Q28 [single] Different shift papers differ in difficulty, so raw scores are normalised (usually to percentile scores); randomisation only shuffles items; negative marking and revaluation do not equate.
- S14-Q41 [two-statement] Computer-based testing logs every action, so response times and answer changes can be analysed; the delivery mode does not improve item quality, so validity must still be established.
- S15-Q49 [single] In CAT the program estimates ability after each response and selects the next item to match, so candidates see different items and fewer are needed. Linear fixed-form gives all the same items; linear-on-the-fly assembles a unique form before the test without adapting.

### Going Further must teach
- S4-Q9 [single] In CBT, randomising questions and options (from a question bank) means neighbours don't see the same item in the same position, a security advantage over a single printed paper.
- S11-Q14 [single] CAT needs item parameters (difficulty, discrimination) on a sample-independent scale so candidates answering different items can be compared; item response theory provides this. Classical test theory statistics are sample-dependent.
- S18-Q49 [single] Multi-shift CBTs report within-shift percentiles; comparing them across shifts assumes candidates of similar ability are distributed across shifts (random allotment); papers differ, so raw scores cannot be compared.

## 6.9 Innovations in evaluation  (26 booklet questions)

### Core must teach
- S1-Q37 [single] A rubric lists criteria and describes performance at each level (excellent, good, fair, poor), making marking transparent and consistent. It is not a question bank, timetable or marks register.
- S1-Q49 [single] A portfolio gathers samples of a student's work (drafts, projects, reflections) over time to show growth and process; an innovation supplementing written exams.
- S2-Q22 [single] In an open-book examination candidates may refer to books/notes, so good questions test understanding, application and analysis rather than memory; an evaluation innovation.
- S3-Q27 [single] In peer assessment students give feedback/marks on classmates' work, usually with a rubric; in self-assessment students judge their own work; both are evaluation innovations.
- S5-Q8 [single] Self-assessment against clear criteria makes learners aware of what they have and haven't mastered (metacognition) and supplements teacher feedback; it is not about ranking or avoiding feedback.
- S6-Q10 [single] An e-portfolio stores a student's selected work electronically with reflections and teacher comments, showing growth over time.
- S7-Q35 [assertion-reason] Performance-based tasks show what students can actually do; they take longer to mark and depend on assessor judgement, hence rubrics; MCQs are quicker and more objective.
- S8-Q11 [single] Remote proctoring supervises online test-takers at their own location via webcam, microphone and screen monitoring (software or human), verifying identity and flagging suspicious behaviour.
- S9-Q24 [single] Peer marks distorted by friendship, rivalry, vague standards; rubrics, anonymity, multiple markers averaged, and training with samples improve reliability.
- S10-Q19 [single] Holistic rubric: describes overall quality levels, single score, quick but little diagnostic feedback; analytic rubric scores each criterion separately; checklist lists features present/absent.
- S11-Q40 [scenario] Authentic assessment uses tasks reproducing real-world conditions (real product, audience, deadline). Portfolio assessment collects work over time; ipsative assessment compares a learner with his or her own earlier performance; objective assessment uses fixed-answer items.
- S12-Q3 [single] Assessing a peer's work against a rubric makes the assessor apply the criteria, clarifying standards of good work and improving his or her own later work; reduced marking load is a benefit to the teacher, not the learner.
- S12-Q23 [single] In on-demand examination the learner chooses when to take the exam and a unique paper is generated from a question bank per a blueprint; suited to open and distance learning (e.g. NIOS). Adaptive testing adjusts difficulty during the test.
- S13-Q25 [single] A rubric has criteria (aspects judged), a scale of performance levels, and descriptors (wording in each cell describing what that level of that criterion looks like), which make scoring consistent.
- S13-Q49 [single] A portfolio is a purposeful collection of selected work against stated criteria, with the student's reflection on why each piece was chosen and what it shows; collecting everything without selection is merely a folder.
- S14-Q34 [single] In open-book examinations recall questions lose value, so questions must demand application, interpretation, analysis and judgement, with books serving as tools, not answer keys.
- S15-Q19 [single] An analytic rubric scores separate criteria and describes levels for each, giving a profile and a total; a holistic rubric gives one overall score; a checklist records only presence or absence; an anecdotal record is a narrative of observed incidents.
- S16-Q16 [two-statement] Self-assessment is mainly reflection against criteria and need not produce a grade. Peer assessment can be confined to comments to improve drafts, without marks.
- S18-Q45 [single] NEP 2020 shifts assessment from summative testing of rote memory to regular, formative, competency-based assessment testing higher-order skills, with holistic progress reporting.

### Going Further must teach
- S4-Q14 [single] Authentic assessment has learners apply knowledge in tasks resembling real life or work (case analyses, projects, presentations, reports for real audiences), revealing what recall tests miss.
- S10-Q37 [single] Portfolio types: working (work in progress), showcase/display (best pieces selected by student), assessment (work showing achievement of objectives).
- S19-Q12 [scenario] Fair group-project marking combines a mark for the group product with peer ratings of each member's contribution against agreed criteria, to reward contribution and discourage free riding.
- S20-Q4 [scenario] Washback is the effect of a test on teaching and learning: negative when it narrows the curriculum (drilling MCQ technique), positive when it encourages desirable learning. Practice effect is score gain from retaking.
- S20-Q14 [numerical] Weighted rubric score = sum of (weight x criterion score); 0.4x4 + 0.3x2 + 0.3x2 = 2.8 of 4 = 70 per cent; unweighted mean ignores weights.
- S20-Q30 [single] Portfolios are strong in authenticity and showing growth but weak in inter-rater agreement; remedies are clear rubrics, rater training, double marking and moderation.
- S20-Q36 [single] Ruth Butler found comments without grades improved learning most; adding grades cancelled the benefit as students attended to the grade. Black and Wiliam recommended comment-only marking.
