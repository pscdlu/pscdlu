
## 1.6 Maxims of teaching and general methods (analytic, synthetic, inductive, deductive)  (19 booklet questions)

### Core must teach
- S2-Q31 [single] Accepted maxims: simple to complex, known to unknown, concrete to abstract, particular to general (also whole to part, induction to deduction, empirical to rational). Reversed forms are wrong.
- S3-Q38 [single] Maxim 'concrete to abstract' means beginning with things learners can see, handle or picture before definitions, rules and theories, which are abstract.
- S4-Q32 [scenario] Linking new material (democracy) to what students already know (class elections) follows 'from known to unknown'; it moves from particular case, so 'general to particular' does not fit.
- S5-Q5 [scenario] Maxim 'whole to part', based on Gestalt psychology: present the complete picture first, then details. Starting with parts and building up is the reverse; 'particular to general' and 'empirical to rational' are other maxims.
- S6-Q46 [single] Psychological order follows learner's interests, needs, development; logical order follows subject structure; teaching moves from former to latter.
- S7-Q17 [scenario] Maxim 'particular to general' underlies inductive method; stating the law first then verifying = 'general to particular' (deductive).
- S8-Q35 [scenario] Maxim 'analysis to synthesis': separate whole into elements to understand, then recombine into meaningful whole; learning incomplete without synthesis.
- S9-Q49 [scenario] Maxim 'concrete to abstract': give tangible experience first, then build abstract concept; starting with definition reverses it.
- S10-Q24 [match] Simple to complex: simple before compound sentences; concrete to abstract: beads before number symbols; whole to part: whole poem before stanzas; particular to general: measured triangles before angle-sum rule.
- S11-Q31 [scenario] 'Empirical to rational' moves from knowledge based on observation and experience to reasoned explanation and proof. 'Particular to general' builds a rule from several cases; 'whole to part' and 'analysis to synthesis' are separate maxims.
- S12-Q11 [scenario] Inferring a rule from examples is induction; applying the rule to new cases is deduction. Good teaching usually combines them, induction first for understanding then deduction for practice.
- S13-Q3 [match] Induction: particular to general; deduction: general to particular; analysis: unknown to known (from what is to be found back to data); synthesis: known to unknown.
- S13-Q41 [multi] Accepted maxims include known to unknown (consistent with Ausubel's prior knowledge), simple to complex (not complex to simple), particular to general (inductive), and indefinite to definite (refining vague ideas into clear ones).
- S15-Q46 [multi] Induction moves from particular examples to a rule and from concrete to abstract; it suits discovery but takes time. Deduction states the rule first and applies it, moving from abstract to concrete, and covers content faster.
- S16-Q13 [two-statement] Concrete to abstract concerns kind of experience (things to ideas); particular to general concerns scope (cases to rule) and is the basis of the inductive method. They are separate maxims.
- S19-Q49 [two-statement] Particular to general corresponds to induction; general to particular (deduction) is equally used in teaching for practice, verification and problem-solving; good teaching combines induction then deduction.

### Going Further must teach
- S6-Q40 [single] Analytic method (maths) starts from what is to be found (unknown) and breaks it down to the given (known); synthetic method moves known to unknown and is shorter; maxim 'analysis to synthesis'.  FLAG: DOUBT: classic maxims list 'known to unknown' as a maxim; guide must distinguish the analytic method (unknown to known) from the maxim known-to-unknown to avoid confusing students. Outline has no clear home for analytic/synthetic methods (placed in 1.6).
- S16-Q39 [scenario] Analytic method proceeds from the unknown (conclusion) back to the known (data), showing why each step is taken; synthetic presents proof from data to conclusion; deduction applies a general rule to cases; induction goes from cases to rule.  FLAG: NEW?: analytic-synthetic method not explicitly in 4.4 list; add to 4.4 or 1.6 (maxim analysis to synthesis).
- S17-Q21 [match] Known to unknown rests on Herbart's apperception; whole to part on Gestalt perception; simple to complex on graded difficulty; particular to general on inductive reasoning.

## 1.7 Characteristics of effective teaching and of a good teacher  (19 booklet questions)

### Core must teach
- S1-Q13 [single] Reflective practice means a teacher looks back on her own teaching, asks what worked and changes future lessons. Subject mastery and verbal fluency describe knowledge or speech, not self-examination.
- S2-Q14 [single] Good teaching has clear objectives and organised plans, encourages interaction and questions, and respects learner differences; teacher-talk dominance, uniform treatment and discouraging questions are features of poor teaching.
- S3-Q9 [single] Good teaching is interactive: teacher explains and asks, learners respond and question, teacher adjusts through feedback; one-way talk gives no feedback on understanding.
- S4-Q29 [scenario] A good teacher reads class feedback and changes pace, example or method; flexibility is a characteristic of effective teaching.
- S5-Q35 [single] Democratic teaching treats learners with respect, gives freedom to ask, differ and participate, and reasons rather than commands; it does not mean abdicating responsibilities or leaving errors uncorrected.
- S6-Q48 [single] Teaching exists to produce learning; success measured by change in learners' knowledge, skills, attitudes, not lectures delivered, notes or silence.
- S7-Q30 [single] Classroom management organises space, time, routines and relationships to maximise engaged learning time and prevent problems; not punishment or silence.
- S7-Q37 [assertion-reason] Classroom communication is carried by voice, face, gesture, eye contact and movement; learners read these cues, so non-verbal behaviour influences learning.
- S8-Q33 [multi] Good teaching provides feedback, links new to known, is flexible to learner responses, and is judged by learning not content coverage.
- S9-Q21 [single] Authoritarian: hard work only under watch, hostility; laissez-faire: little work; democratic: work continued in leader's absence, friendly and satisfied members.
- S10-Q47 [assertion-reason] Teaching is two-way; feedback from questions, expressions, tests lets teacher match pace, examples and method to learners' understanding.
- S12-Q2 [assertion-reason] Good teaching locates learners' difficulties (diagnostic) and helps overcome them (remedial), precisely because learners differ in pace, style and background; it does not assume all learn alike.
- S14-Q15 [two-statement] Good teaching is judged by the learning it produces, not syllabus coverage, and is flexible and democratic, adapting methods to learners rather than rigid and authoritarian.
- S17-Q27 [assertion-reason] Good teaching gives guidance suited to learners' needs so that it can be withdrawn; its aim is an independent learner, not permanent dependence on the teacher.

### Going Further must teach
- S14-Q36 [scenario] Kounin: withitness is communicating awareness of everything happening in the room; overlapping is attending to two matters at once; momentum and smoothness concern lesson flow.
- S15-Q11 [single] In Mitzel's and Dunkin and Biddle's model, presage variables are what teachers bring (formative experiences, training, traits); context variables concern pupils, school and community; process variables are observable classroom behaviours; product variables are pupil outcomes.
- S19-Q32 [single] Pass percentage is a product measure; student outcomes also depend on entry level, motivation, class size and facilities beyond the teacher's control, so value-added or contextualised measures are fairer.
- S19-Q46 [single] Seven principles: student-faculty contact, cooperation among students, active learning, prompt feedback, time on task, high expectations, respect for diverse talents and ways of learning.
- S20-Q20 [scenario] Teacher immediacy (Mehrabian): verbal and non-verbal behaviours (smiling, eye contact, names, moving among students) reducing psychological distance; clarity is intelligibility of explanation; credibility is perceived competence and trustworthiness; efficacy is belief in one's capability.

## 1.8 Basic requirements of teaching: teaching skills, micro-teaching and classroom interaction  (43 booklet questions)

### Core must teach
- S1-Q5 [single] Micro-teaching was developed at Stanford University in 1963 by Dwight W. Allen and colleagues for controlled practice of single skills with immediate feedback; later adapted in India.
- S1-Q26 [single] Micro-teaching is a scaled-down teaching encounter in teacher training: short lesson (5-10 min) to a small group, one skill at a time, feedback, re-teach. It is unrelated to pupils' age or miniature models.
- S2-Q12 [single] Micro-teaching breaks teaching into component skills (questioning, explaining, reinforcement); trainee practises one skill per short lesson, gets feedback and re-teaches; skills are later integrated.
- S2-Q47 [single] Basic requirements of teaching include clear communication, subject knowledge, planning and understanding learners; costly equipment is not essential; silence or unbroken lecture means no interaction.
- S3-Q47 [single] Micro-teaching scales down class (5-10 pupils), time (5-10 minutes) and task (one skill); a normal period is 40-45 minutes.
- S4-Q2 [single] Teacher competence consists of knowing the subject and how to teach it (planning, explaining, questioning, managing class, assessing); seniority, appearance and administrative post do not improve learning.
- S4-Q8 [single] Set induction (introducing a lesson) arouses interest and connects topic with prior knowledge; closure consolidates at the end; stimulus variation holds attention; reinforcement encourages desired responses.
- S4-Q39 [single] Micro-teaching reduces class to about 5-10 pupils (real pupils or peers), with a short lesson and single skill.
- S5-Q11 [single] Micro-teaching scales down class size, lesson length and number of skills, but feedback is central, immediate and detailed (supervisor, peers, video).
- S6-Q19 [scenario] Reinforcement skill: encouraging desired responses with praise, nods, smiles; probing = follow-up questions; illustrating = examples; closure = consolidating at end.
- S6-Q45 [single] Flanders: ten categories, coded every three seconds (teacher talk, student talk, silence/confusion); shows direct vs indirect influence; verbal behaviour only.
- S7-Q10 [scenario] Probing questioning: prompting, seeking clarification, refocusing, redirecting after a partial answer; reinforcement praises; explaining has teacher supply content; stimulus variation changes cues.
- S7-Q21 [numerical] Indian model (Passi): teach 6, feedback 6, re-plan 12, re-teach 6, re-feedback 6 minutes = 36 minutes; peers as pupils.
- S7-Q44 [scenario] Basic requirement 'knowing the learner' = finding out prior knowledge, differences and needs and fitting the lesson; finishing early, dictating notes, hard papers do not show it.
- S8-Q6 [single] Flanders: category recorded every three seconds (about 20 a minute), entered in a 10x10 matrix to compute teacher talk, student talk, indirect/direct ratios.
- S9-Q12 [single] Simulated teaching: small group of trainees practise among themselves, one teaches, others act as pupils and observers, roles rotated.
- S9-Q41 [scenario] Micro-teaching trains one skill at a time; link practice (integration of skills) combines skills in intermediate-length lessons with intermediate groups before real classroom.
- S9-Q46 [match] Set induction links with previous knowledge; stimulus variation shifts sensory channels; reinforcement uses praise and nods; closure summarises main points.
- S10-Q7 [single] Flanders: categories 1-7 teacher talk (1-4 indirect, 5-7 direct), 8-9 student talk (response, initiation), 10 silence/confusion.
- S11-Q12 [assertion-reason] A teacher must assess learners' entry behaviour so new learning begins where learners are; subject mastery is a separate basic requirement and does not explain the need to study learners.
- S13-Q13 [multi] Basic requirements of teaching include competence in content, communication skill, understanding of learners and methods. Fair evaluation requires transparency and accountability, not unchecked authority to grade.
- S13-Q38 [single] Putting the question to the whole class, pausing, then naming a student keeps every student thinking; naming first lets others relax. Components of questioning include clarity, relevance, pausing and distribution of questions.
- S15-Q10 [single] FIAC has ten categories: indirect teacher talk (1 accepts feelings, 2 praises/encourages, 3 accepts or uses students' ideas, 4 asks questions); direct teacher talk (5 lecturing, 6 giving directions, 7 criticising/justifying authority); student talk (8 response, 9 initiation); 10 silence or confusion.
- S15-Q26 [scenario] Stimulus variation components: teacher movement, gestures, change in speech pattern, pausing, focusing, change in interaction style, oral-visual switching. Set induction secures attention only at lesson opening by linking to previous knowledge.
- S15-Q47 [sequence] Micro-teaching cycle: plan, teach, feedback, re-plan, re-teach, re-feedback; re-teaching is usually to a different group of pupils.
- S16-Q2 [two-statement] Subject knowledge alone is insufficient; it must be joined to pedagogical skill. Knowing learners' previous knowledge, abilities and interests is equally a requirement of teaching.
- S17-Q26 [multi] FIACS: categories 1-4 indirect teacher influence (4 = asking questions), 5-7 direct influence, 8-9 student talk, 10 silence/confusion. Only verbal behaviour is coded.
- S18-Q44 [single] Convergent questions have one correct answer (recall or computation); divergent questions have many acceptable answers and call for prediction, imagination and reasoning, typically higher-order.
- S20-Q3 [match] Probing deepens a student's response; illustrating with examples clarifies abstract ideas; stimulus variation sustains attention; closure consolidates learning at the end.
- S20-Q21 [scenario] Useful feedback is specific, descriptive, tied to the skill practised and points to a concrete next step; general praise or blame gives nothing to act on.
- S20-Q25 [multi] Basic requirements include content mastery, knowledge of learners' psychology and communication skill; a belief that ability is fixed lowers expectations and is not a requirement.

### Going Further must teach
- S9-Q29 [single] Shulman's PCK: blend of content and pedagogy - best examples, analogies, explanations for a topic and typical learner difficulties; distinct from subject knowledge and general skill.
- S11-Q19 [single] TPACK (Mishra and Koehler, 2006) describes teacher knowledge at the intersection of technology, pedagogy and content, extending Shulman's PCK. SAMR classifies levels of technology use; ADDIE and ASSURE are instructional design models.
- S11-Q26 [scenario] Components of the skill of explaining include explaining links (because, therefore, hence, as a result), clarity, fluency, beginning and concluding statements, and testing understanding. Planned repetition and stimulus variation and set induction are other skills/components serving different purposes.
- S11-Q32 [scenario] Micro-teaching phases: knowledge acquisition (reading, discussion, observing a model lesson), skill acquisition (plan, teach, feedback, re-plan, re-teach), and transfer (using the skill in real classroom teaching).
- S12-Q41 [two-statement] Mary Budd Rowe found that waiting three seconds or more after a question brings longer, more thoughtful answers from more students. Calling only on volunteers concentrates participation in a few students; questions should be distributed across the class.
- S12-Q45 [numerical] In Flanders' FIAC, categories 1-4 are indirect teacher talk and 5-7 direct; I/D ratio = tallies in 1-4 ÷ tallies in 5-7 (e.g. 120/240 = 0.5). A ratio below 1 means more direct influence.
- S13-Q9 [single] In micro-teaching, a video recording gives the most objective feedback because it can be replayed and is free from observer memory and bias; supervisor and peer comments are useful but filtered through perception.
- S14-Q32 [single] Flanders' rule of two-thirds: about two-thirds of class time someone is talking; two-thirds of that talk is teacher talk; two-thirds of teacher talk is direct influence (lecturing, directing, criticising).
- S16-Q20 [scenario] Probing components: prompting (hints after no/wrong answer), seeking further information, refocusing (relating answer to another situation), redirection (same question to other students), increasing critical awareness (asking for justification).
- S16-Q37 [single] Stanford model used real pupils and videotape feedback. Indian model (Passi/NCERT) uses peers as pupils under simulated conditions and feedback from supervisors and peer observers, without costly equipment; full cycle and one-skill principle retained.
- S18-Q24 [numerical] Indian micro-teaching cycle: teach 6, feedback 6, re-plan 12, re-teach 6, re-feedback 6 = 36 minutes; 180 minutes allows 5 cycles.
- S19-Q42 [numerical] Teacher talk = categories 1-7 (indirect 1-4 plus direct 5-7); student talk 8-9; silence/confusion 10. Teacher talk % = (1-7 tallies)/total x 100, e.g. 450/600 = 75 per cent.

## 1.9 Models of teaching  (10 booklet questions)

### Core must teach
- S6-Q21 [single] Joyce & Weil: Models of Teaching, four families (information-processing, social, personal, behavioural); Flanders/Amidon: interaction analysis; Allen/Ryan: micro-teaching; Bloom/Krathwohl: taxonomy.
- S8-Q29 [single] Joyce & Weil: Concept Attainment (Bruner), Inquiry Training (Suchman), Advance Organiser (Ausubel) belong to information-processing family; other families social, personal, behavioural systems.
- S9-Q10 [single] Joyce and Weil model elements: focus, syntax (phases), social system (roles, relationships), principles of reaction (teacher responses), support system (materials).
- S16-Q1 [match] Concept attainment model is based on Bruner; inquiry training on Suchman; advance organiser on Ausubel; jurisprudential inquiry on Oliver and Shaver.
- S19-Q21 [match] Joyce and Weil describe models by focus (central aim), syntax (sequence of phases), social system (roles and relationships), principles of reaction, and support system (special materials and facilities).

### Going Further must teach
- S10-Q28 [single] Gagné's nine events: gain attention, inform objective, stimulate recall, present stimulus, provide guidance, elicit performance, give feedback, assess performance, enhance retention and transfer.
- S11-Q16 [match] Information-processing family includes Suchman's inquiry training; social family includes Thelen's group investigation; personal family includes Rogers's non-directive teaching; behavioural family includes contingency management (Skinner).
- S13-Q31 [single] Synectics (William Gordon), a personal-family model, develops creativity by 'making the strange familiar and the familiar strange' using direct analogy, personal analogy and compressed conflict. Inquiry training (Suchman) trains investigation of puzzling events; concept attainment (Bruner) uses examples and non-examples; jurisprudential inquiry addresses public policy issues.
- S13-Q40 [single] Robert Glaser's Basic Teaching Model: instructional objectives, entering behaviour, instructional procedures, performance assessment, linked by feedback loops.
- S18-Q14 [scenario] Concept attainment (Bruner) phases: presentation of examples and non-examples, testing attainment, analysis of thinking strategies; it is in the information-processing family, as are inquiry training and advance organiser.

## 1.10 Professional ethics and the teacher's professional growth  (8 booklet questions)

### Core must teach
- S5-Q30 [single] A profession is marked by specialised knowledge, long training, service motive and a code of conduct; salary, post and facilities are conditions of employment, not marks of a profession.
- S8-Q50 [scenario] Professional ethics require just, impartial treatment of all students especially in assessment and discourage private tuition creating conflict of interest.
- S10-Q42 [scenario] Teachers must keep students' personal information private unless disclosure protects the student or others; betrayal damages trust in guidance and counselling.
- S12-Q31 [scenario] Using a colleague's work without acknowledgement is plagiarism, a breach of intellectual honesty and professional ethics towards colleagues. Academic freedom protects the right to teach, research and publish but gives no right over others' work.
- S14-Q11 [scenario] The code of professional ethics requires teachers to perform examination duties (setting, invigilation, evaluation) with honesty and confidentiality; leaking papers is a serious breach, with no exemption for relatives. Teacher autonomy does not cover disclosure.
- S17-Q11 [single] Academic freedom protects inquiry, expression of evidence-based (even controversial) views, criticism of theories and choice of method, but does not permit disregarding the prescribed curriculum or evaluation norms.

### Going Further must teach
- S18-Q36 [assertion-reason] NEP 2020 expects continuous professional development for teachers (50 hours a year) and sets the four-year integrated B.Ed. as minimum qualification for school teachers by 2030; these are separate provisions.  FLAG: NEW?: NEP 2020 teacher-education provisions not explicit in outline; place in 1.10 or 1.8.
- S19-Q50 [single] Gift/honorary authorship lists a non-contributor as author; ghost authorship omits a real contributor; salami slicing splits one study into many papers; self-plagiarism reuses one's own published work without citation. ## Booklet 0 (diagnostic) questions the guide must also teach
- P2-Q22 -> 1.8: answer and explanation: (A)**, because the micro-teaching cycle runs: plan a short lesson (2), teach it to a small group (4), receive feedback (3), re-plan in the light of that feedback (5) and re-teach (1), after which feedback is given again. Feedback must come before re-planning, which rules out the option that places re-planning ahead of it. 
- P3-Q14 -> 1.6: answer and explanation: (C)**, because the accepted maxims lead the learner from the known to the unknown (1), from the concrete to the abstract (3) and from the particular to the general (4). Statements 2 and 5 reverse two other maxims: teaching should move from the simple to the complex, and from the near to the far.
