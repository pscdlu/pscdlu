
## 2.1 The learner in higher education: adolescence and early adulthood  (14 booklet questions)

### Core must teach
- S1-Q27 [single] Erikson's adolescent stage is identity vs role confusion. Trust vs mistrust is infancy, industry vs inferiority school age, integrity vs despair old age.
- S2-Q44 [single] G. Stanley Hall described adolescence as a period of storm and stress due to rapid growth, emotional swings, identity search and conflict with authority.
- S4-Q45 [single] Adolescents idealise heroes (sports stars, leaders, teachers) and day-dream about future roles, linked to identity search and emotional intensity.
- S5-Q9 [single] Students join college at about 17-18, late adolescence: capable of abstract thought, forming identity, peer-influenced; PG and research students are mostly young adults.
- S6-Q32 [single] Adolescents are self-conscious, peer-sensitive, emotionally changeable and capable of abstract thought; correct them privately.
- S10-Q34 [single] Havighurst: developmental tasks society expects at each stage; adolescence tasks include accepting one's body, emotional independence, mature peer relations, career preparation; Erikson: psychosocial stages; Hall: storm and stress.
- S14-Q23 [multi] Adolescents are capable of abstract thinking (formal operations), seek independence from parents, are concerned about body image, and show emotional intensity and fluctuation (not stability).
- S15-Q31 [multi] Piaget: hypothetico-deductive reasoning appears in formal operations. Erikson: adolescence is identity vs role confusion; intimacy vs isolation belongs to young adulthood. Peer influence increases in adolescence.
- S16-Q12 [scenario] Moratorium = exploration without commitment; diffusion = neither; foreclosure = commitment without exploration; achievement = commitment after exploration.
- S19-Q40 [assertion-reason] Identity formation (Erikson, Marcia) continues into the early twenties among university students; Kohlberg's stages concern moral reasoning, a separate aspect of development.
- S20-Q42 [single] Jeffrey Arnett (2000) proposed emerging adulthood (about 18-25) marked by identity exploration, instability and feeling in-between. Erikson: identity crisis; Marcia: identity statuses; Levinson: seasons of adult life.

### Going Further must teach
- S7-Q36 [single] Elkind's adolescent egocentrism: imaginary audience (being always on stage, self-consciousness) and personal fable (uniqueness, invulnerability, risk-taking).
- S9-Q31 [scenario] Marcia's statuses: diffusion (no exploration, no commitment), foreclosure (commitment without exploration), moratorium (exploration without commitment), achievement (commitment after exploration).
- S12-Q18 [scenario] Elkind's adolescent egocentrism has two forms: the personal fable (belief in being unique and invulnerable, leading to risk-taking) and the imaginary audience (feeling constantly watched). Foreclosure and moratorium are Marcia's identity statuses.

## 2.2 Cognitive characteristics: how the learner thinks  (49 booklet questions)

### Core must teach
- S1-Q8 [single] Vygotsky's ZPD is the gap between what a learner can do alone and with help from a teacher or more capable peer. Piaget (stages), Bruner (modes, discovery) and Ausubel (advance organisers) are distinct.
- S1-Q40 [single] Piaget's four cognitive stages: sensorimotor, pre-operational, concrete operational, formal operational (abstract reasoning from ~11-12). Psychosocial = Erikson; operant conditioning = Skinner; multiple intelligences = Gardner.
- S2-Q2 [single] Scaffolding is help (hints, prompts, models, questions) from teacher or capable peer to do a task just beyond current ability, removed gradually as the learner becomes independent.
- S2-Q16 [single] Piaget's stages: sensorimotor (0-2), pre-operational (2-7), concrete operational (7-11), formal operational (11+).
- S2-Q26 [single] Bruner held learners understand best what they discover themselves. Ausubel = meaningful reception learning; Gagné = conditions of learning; Bandura = observational learning.
- S3-Q19 [single] For Vygotsky learning is first social then individual, through interaction with adults and capable peers with language as chief tool (social constructivism). Reinforcement and trial and error are behaviourist.
- S3-Q42 [single] Ausubel proposed advance organisers, brief general introductions presented before detailed content to give a framework, central to meaningful verbal learning. Bruner = discovery; Gagné = conditions of learning; Bandura = observational learning.
- S3-Q48 [single] From about 11-12 years (formal operational) young people reason about possibilities and hypotheses; concrete operational thinking is tied to real objects.
- S4-Q5 [single] Bruner's modes of representation: enactive (action), iconic (images), symbolic (language/symbols), usually taught in that order. VAK are learning styles; receiving/responding/valuing are affective levels; preparation/presentation/application are Herbartian steps.
- S4-Q16 [single] Piaget: assimilation interprets new experience via an existing schema; accommodation changes the schema; equilibration balances the two; conservation is a concrete-operational achievement.
- S4-Q36 [single] The More Knowledgeable Other (teacher, parent, capable peer or tool) supports the learner within the ZPD. 'Generalised other' is G. H. Mead's term; advance organiser is Ausubel's.
- S5-Q31 [single] Vygotsky saw language as the chief cultural tool: social speech becomes private speech guiding action, then silent inner speech (thought).
- S5-Q36 [single] Conservation (concrete operational) is understanding quantity/number/mass stays same despite appearance change. Egocentrism and animism are pre-operational; object permanence develops in sensorimotor stage.
- S5-Q49 [single] Ausubel: most classroom learning is reception of verbal material, meaningful when related to existing knowledge (contrasted with rote). Discovery = Bruner; operant = Skinner; observational = Bandura.
- S6-Q5 [single] Piaget = cognitive (individual) constructivism; Vygotsky = social/socio-cultural constructivism stressing interaction with others and culture.
- S6-Q50 [single] Pre-operational (2-7 yrs): egocentrism; reversibility appears in concrete operational; abstract reasoning and hypothesis testing in formal operational.
- S7-Q13 [scenario] Concrete operational (about 7-11): classification, seriation, conservation on real objects; hypothetical-deductive reasoning needs formal operations; age is only a rough guide.
- S7-Q22 [single] Bruner: any subject can be taught in honest form at any stage; curriculum returns to basic ideas at higher levels; enactive, iconic, symbolic modes.
- S8-Q21 [scenario] Vygotsky: actual development = unaided performance; ZPD = what can be done with guidance; beyond ZPD = not even with help; instruction most fruitful in ZPD.
- S8-Q22 [single] Equilibration is Piaget's self-regulating process resolving disequilibrium through assimilation and accommodation; conservation, seriation and centration are stage features.
- S9-Q22 [scenario] Bruner's modes: enactive (action), iconic (images, diagrams), symbolic (words, formulae), recommended in that order.
- S10-Q4 [multi] Piaget's stage sequence is invariant (ages vary); egocentrism marks pre-operational; child is active constructor; formal operations begin about 11-12 years.
- S11-Q46 [assertion-reason] Vygotsky defined the zone of proximal development as the gap between what a learner can do alone and with help from adults or more capable peers; a slightly more advanced peer tutor can scaffold within this zone, aiding the tutee's cognitive development.
- S12-Q40 [assertion-reason] Bruner rejected waiting for readiness, claiming any subject can be taught in some intellectually honest form to any child at any stage (spiral curriculum). His modes of representation are enactive, iconic and symbolic.
- S13-Q27 [scenario] Centration is the pre-operational child's focus on one striking feature (e.g. height of water) neglecting others, causing failure on conservation tasks. Conservation, reversibility and seriation appear in the concrete operational stage.
- S13-Q42 [two-statement] Vygotsky held that good learning, aimed at the ZPD, runs ahead of and leads development. Piaget held that the developmental stage reached largely sets the limits of what can be learnt.
- S15-Q27 [scenario] Assimilation fits a new experience into an existing schema (calling a cow a 'big dog'); accommodation changes a schema or creates a new one when experience does not fit (forming the category 'cow').
- S16-Q48 [single] Actual development = independent problem-solving; potential development = problem-solving with guidance from a more capable person; ZPD is the distance between the two.
- S16-Q50 [assertion-reason] Pre-operational children fail conservation tasks; conservation is the chief achievement of the concrete operational stage and depends on understanding reversibility.
- S18-Q4 [assertion-reason] Piaget's formal operational stage frees thought from the concrete: reasoning about propositions, possibilities and hypotheses. Dependence on concrete objects marks the concrete operational stage.
- S18-Q6 [scenario] Miller: short-term/working memory holds about seven plus or minus two items; chunking groups items into meaningful units to stay within capacity. Interference explains forgetting; the sensory register is brief.
- S18-Q34 [assertion-reason] Vygotsky held that higher mental functions appear first between people (social plane) and then within the individual; hence classroom dialogue is central.
- S18-Q42 [multi] Meaningful material is retained better than nonsense material; distributed practice beats massed practice; overlearning improves retention; Ebbinghaus's curve shows forgetting fastest soon after learning.
- S19-Q8 [assertion-reason] Mnemonic devices (acronyms, rhymes, images) aid retention of unrelated facts by imposing artificial organisation that supplies retrieval cues.
- S19-Q29 [single] Bruner's modes of representation: enactive (action/objects), iconic (images/pictures), symbolic (language/notation); the concrete-pictorial-abstract sequence follows this order.
- S20-Q2 [scenario] Two children with the same actual development can have different zones (e.g. one year vs four years); instruction should target the zone, so they need different teaching; unaided tests hide the difference.

### Going Further must teach
- S9-Q11 [scenario] Ausubel: comparative organiser relates new material to familiar knowledge (similarities, differences); expository organiser gives framework for wholly new material.
- S11-Q35 [single] Ausubel saw cognitive structure as hierarchical; meaningful learning occurs when new specific material is subsumed under an existing inclusive concept (subsumer); advance organisers supply or activate subsumers. Accommodation is Piaget's term for modifying a schema.
- S11-Q36 [assertion-reason] Formal operational thought (from about 11-12) involves abstract and hypothetical reasoning. Piaget's ages are approximate, and he later accepted that many adolescents and adults use formal reasoning only in familiar fields, not uniformly.
- S13-Q5 [single] Ausubel's conditions: logically meaningful material, relevant anchoring ideas in cognitive structure, and a meaningful learning set (intention to relate new to known). Intending only verbatim memorising makes learning rote; reception (lecture) learning can be meaningful.
- S14-Q26 [two-statement] Bruner's enactive mode represents through action, iconic through images, symbolic through language/symbols. 'Scaffolding' was introduced by Wood, Bruner and Ross (1976) and later linked to Vygotsky's ZPD.
- S15-Q9 [assertion-reason] Ausubel treated reception vs discovery and rote vs meaningful as independent dimensions: reception (expository) learning can be meaningful and discovery can be rote. Learning is meaningful when new material is substantively related to existing cognitive structure.
- S17-Q23 [two-statement] Bruner's theory of instruction (Toward a Theory of Instruction) addresses predisposition to learn, structure of knowledge, sequence and reinforcement. He held any subject can be taught in some honest form to any child, so discovery is not excluded for young children.
- S17-Q47 [scenario] Progressive differentiation: present most general, inclusive ideas first, then detail. Integrative reconciliation: explicitly relate new ideas to earlier ones and resolve contradictions.
- S18-Q5 [scenario] Maintenance rehearsal is plain repetition to hold items in short-term memory; elaborative rehearsal links items to existing knowledge (deep processing, Craik and Lockhart) and aids long-term retention.
- S18-Q10 [scenario] Piaget saw children's self-talk as egocentric speech showing immaturity that fades; Vygotsky saw it as private speech that guides and regulates thought and later becomes inner speech.
- S19-Q33 [scenario] Ausubel crossed reception vs discovery (how content becomes available) with rote vs meaningful (how it is incorporated). Discovery without understanding is rote discovery learning.
- S20-Q1 [single] Serial position effect: better recall of first (primacy) and last (recency) items, worst for middle. Von Restorff: distinctive item recalled better; Zeigarnik: unfinished tasks recalled better; Hawthorne: change due to being observed.
- S20-Q40 [multi] Critiques: Piaget underestimated young children; gave less weight to social-cultural influence than Vygotsky; stage ages vary with culture and schooling (not fixed); not all adults use formal operations in all fields.

## 2.3 How learners learn: theories of learning  (36 booklet questions)

### Core must teach
- S1-Q42 [single] Skinner: behaviour shaped by consequences; reinforced responses repeat (operant conditioning); programmed instruction applies it. Pavlov = classical conditioning; Köhler = insight; Bandura = observational learning.
- S1-Q45 [single] John Dewey, progressive education, held that learners learn through active experience and reflection; basis of project and problem-solving methods. Herbart, Pavlov, Spearman are unrelated.
- S2-Q28 [single] Pavlov paired a bell with food until the bell alone elicited salivation (classical conditioning). Skinner = operant; Thorndike = trial and error; Lewin = field theory/leadership.
- S3-Q28 [single] Thorndike's law of effect: satisfying results strengthen a response, annoying ones weaken it; with laws of readiness and exercise forms trial-and-error learning (connectionism). Pavlov = classical; Köhler and Koffka = Gestalt/insight.
- S4-Q40 [single] Thorndike's puzzle-box cats learned by trial and error, stamping in correct S-R connections (connectionism). Insight = Köhler; classical conditioning = Pavlov; social learning = Bandura.
- S5-Q43 [single] Positive reinforcement adds something pleasant to strengthen a response; negative reinforcement removes something unpleasant to strengthen it; punishment weakens; extinction is fading when reinforcement stops.
- S6-Q28 [single] Bandura's Bobo doll: children imitated an aggressive model; learning by observing models (social learning theory); trial and error = Thorndike; insight = Köhler; paired stimuli = Pavlov.
- S6-Q34 [single] Pavlov: food = UCS, salivation to food = UCR, bell after pairing = CS, salivation to bell = CR.
- S7-Q4 [scenario] Köhler (Gestalt): sudden solution by perceiving whole situation and relations = insight; trial and error = Thorndike; operant = Skinner; latent learning = Tolman.
- S7-Q29 [scenario] Extinction: behaviour weakens when its reinforcer is withheld; punishment applies something unpleasant or removes pleasant; negative reinforcement removes aversive to strengthen behaviour; spontaneous recovery is reappearance after extinction.
- S8-Q38 [sequence] Gagné's eight types: signal, stimulus-response, chaining, verbal association, discrimination, concept, rule (principle), problem solving; each prerequisite for the next.
- S8-Q48 [scenario] Ratio schedules depend on number of responses, interval schedules on time; fixed-ratio = set number; variable-ratio = varying number around average; fixed/variable interval by time.
- S9-Q13 [sequence] Bandura: attention, retention, reproduction (motor), motivation; reinforcement (direct or vicarious) mainly affects motivation.
- S9-Q43 [single] Thorndike's laws: readiness, exercise (use strengthens, disuse weakens), effect (satisfying consequences strengthen); belongingness is a later subsidiary law; exercise later qualified by knowledge of results.
- S10-Q35 [single] Gestalt psychology (Wertheimer, Köhler, Koffka): perceiving and learning organised wholes, insight; behaviourism and connectionism analyse into S-R units; structuralism analyses consciousness into elements.
- S11-Q43 [scenario] Vicarious reinforcement: an observer's behaviour increases after seeing a model rewarded. Direct reinforcement rewards the learner herself; self-reinforcement is rewarding oneself for meeting one's own standard; negative reinforcement removes an aversive stimulus.
- S12-Q9 [scenario] Punishment reduces behaviour: positive punishment adds something unpleasant; negative punishment (response cost) removes something pleasant. Reinforcement increases behaviour; negative reinforcement removes something unpleasant. Extinction withholds reinforcement.
- S14-Q12 [scenario] A neutral stimulus (classroom) paired with an unconditioned stimulus (humiliation) becomes a conditioned stimulus evoking anxiety; involuntary emotional reactions are classically conditioned. Operant conditioning shapes voluntary behaviour by consequences.
- S15-Q29 [scenario] Reinforcement increases behaviour, punishment decreases it; positive adds, negative removes. Removing an unpleasant event (a tedious test) when the behaviour occurs, so the behaviour rises, is negative reinforcement.
- S15-Q45 [match] Bandura: observational learning. Köhler: insight learning (chimpanzee studies). Thorndike: law of effect. Tolman: latent learning (learning without reinforcement, shown later).
- S16-Q7 [scenario] Stimulus generalisation: a response conditioned to one stimulus is evoked by similar stimuli. Discrimination is responding only to the original stimulus; response generalisation is one stimulus evoking several similar responses; spontaneous recovery is return of an extinguished response.
- S16-Q28 [assertion-reason] Constructivism: learners build knowledge by testing existing ideas against experience; errors reveal those ideas and are opportunities for restructuring understanding.
- S17-Q17 [single] Law of readiness: when ready, acting satisfies and not acting annoys; when not ready, forced action annoys. Law of effect: consequences after a response; law of exercise: use and disuse.
- S19-Q6 [assertion-reason] Bandura showed behaviour is acquired by observing a model without reinforcement; reinforcement (direct or vicarious) mainly affects performance of the acquired behaviour.

### Going Further must teach
- S10-Q17 [scenario] Premack principle ('Grandma's rule'): high-frequency behaviour reinforces low-frequency behaviour; shaping = successive approximations; token economy = exchangeable tokens.
- S11-Q33 [scenario] Shaping reinforces successive approximations to a target behaviour, raising the requirement gradually. Chaining links separate responses into a sequence; modelling is learning by imitation; fading gradually withdraws prompts.
- S12-Q5 [single] Lewin's field theory: behaviour is a function of the person and the psychological environment as perceived (together the life space); learning is change in cognitive structure of the life space. Practice and effect belong to Thorndike's connectionism.
- S12-Q16 [match] Gagné's learned capabilities: verbal information (stating facts), intellectual skills (using rules and concepts), cognitive strategies (managing own learning/thinking), motor skills, attitudes (choices of personal action).
- S13-Q17 [single] Variable-ratio schedules produce responding most resistant to extinction because the end of reinforcement is not noticed. Continuous reinforcement gives fastest acquisition but fastest extinction; fixed-ratio and fixed-interval schedules are more predictable.
- S14-Q19 [single] Bandura's reciprocal determinism: personal factors (beliefs, expectations), behaviour and environment influence one another; this triadic interaction is central to social cognitive theory, rejecting one-way environmental or inner-drive determination.
- S14-Q31 [scenario] Tolman argued rats learnt the maze layout as a cognitive map (sign learning, purposive behaviourism), not a fixed chain of habits, which is why they took a new route when the usual path was blocked.
- S16-Q46 [sequence] Gagné's events: gaining attention, informing learners of objective, stimulating recall, presenting stimulus, providing guidance, eliciting performance, providing feedback, assessing performance, enhancing retention and transfer.
- S18-Q8 [match] Gaining attention supports reception; informing objective creates expectancy; learning guidance supports semantic encoding; feedback provides reinforcement.
- S19-Q41 [scenario] Points given at once after correct responses are immediate positive reinforcement; rewards after an unpredictable number of responses form a variable-ratio schedule producing persistent responding. Negative reinforcement removes something unpleasant; fixed-interval rewards after set time.
- S20-Q12 [single] Connectivism (Siemens and Downes) sees learning as creating and navigating networks of people, sources and devices; learning may reside in non-human appliances; capacity to know more matters more than what is known. Underlies cMOOCs.
- S20-Q16 [scenario] Gagné's hierarchy: rule learning requires mastery of component concepts; problem solving lies above rules. Task analysis works downward from the target skill to prerequisites.

## 2.4 Academic characteristics: learning styles, study habits and self-regulation  (12 booklet questions)

### Core must teach
- S1-Q16 [single] Neil Fleming's VARK = Visual, Auditory/Aural, Read/Write, Kinaesthetic; visual learners prefer diagrams, charts and maps.
- S7-Q18 [single] Kolb's cycle: concrete experience, reflective observation, abstract conceptualisation, active experimentation; styles diverging, assimilating, converging, accommodating.
- S8-Q37 [match] Fleming's VARK: Visual - diagrams, flow charts; Aural - listening, discussion; Read/Write - lists, notes; Kinaesthetic - practice, field experiments.
- S14-Q5 [assertion-reason] Teachers should vary methods and media because classes are diverse and content demands different modes. Learners have preferences, not exclusive channels; fixed learning styles are not supported by evidence.
- S17-Q44 [match] Diverger: concrete experience + reflective observation; assimilator: abstract conceptualisation + reflective observation; converger: abstract conceptualisation + active experimentation; accommodator: concrete experience + active experimentation.

### Going Further must teach
- S9-Q42 [single] Witkin: field-independent learners separate items from background, analytical, self-directed; field-dependent learners perceive globally, attend to social cues, prefer group work and guidance.
- S10-Q14 [single] Kolb styles: converger (AC+AE, practical application), diverger (CE+RO, imaginative), assimilator (AC+RO, theory building), accommodator (CE+AE, hands-on, intuitive).
- S11-Q17 [two-statement] Neil Fleming developed VARK (1987). Research reviews (e.g. Pashler et al. 2008) find little sound evidence that matching teaching to a diagnosed style raises achievement; varying methods for all is the defensible practice.
- S12-Q28 [scenario] Kagan distinguished impulsive learners (fast, inaccurate) from reflective learners (slow, accurate), measured by the Matching Familiar Figures Test. Field independence (Witkin) concerns separating figure from background; convergent thinking (Guilford) seeks one right answer.
- S13-Q15 [single] Honey and Mumford's styles, linked to Kolb's cycle: activists plunge into experience; reflectors stand back, gather data and observe before concluding; theorists integrate observations into logical theories; pragmatists try ideas out in practice.
- S18-Q40 [two-statement] Kolb built his cycle on Dewey, Lewin and Piaget with reflection on experience central. Honey and Mumford: activist-concrete experience, reflector-reflective observation, theorist-abstract conceptualisation, pragmatist-active experimentation.
- S20-Q43 [assertion-reason] Kolb derived four styles from preferences for stages of the experiential cycle; they are relatively stable but can change with personality, education and career; mature learners integrate all modes.

- S17-Q8 [scenario] Kohlberg: pre-conventional (punishment, self-interest), conventional (good boy/girl, law and order stage 4), post-conventional (social contract, universal principles that may override rules). Heteronomous is Piaget's term.

## Booklet 0 (diagnostic) questions the guide must also teach
- P1-Q22 -> 2.3: answer and explanation: (D)**, because Pavlov demonstrated classical conditioning in his experiments with dogs (1-III), Skinner developed operant conditioning based on reinforcement (2-IV), Köhler showed insight learning in his work with chimpanzees (3-I) and Bandura proposed observational, or social, learning (4-II). Pavlov and classical conditioning is the anchor pair; swapping Pavlov with Skinner is the commonest confusion. 
- P2-Q30 -> 2.2: answer and explanation: (D)**, because Statement I is false: the zone of proximal development is the gap between what a learner can do alone and what the learner can do with guidance from a more capable person. Statement II is true: scaffolding is temporary support that is withdrawn as competence grows. Statement I tempts because it describes the learner's actual level of development, the lower boundary of the zone. UGC NET Paper 1 · Booklet 0 · Where Do I Stand? · Page 37 of 93 
- P4-Q22 -> 2.2: answer and explanation: (D)**, because Piaget's stages run: sensorimotor, from birth to about two years (2); pre-operational, from about two to seven (4); concrete operational, from about seven to eleven (1); and formal operational, from about eleven onwards (3). 'Pre-operational' must come before the two operational stages, and concrete thinking develops before abstract, formal thinking. 
- P4-Q38 -> 2.2: answer and explanation: (D)**, because Ausubel's advance organiser is a general introduction presented before the new material, which gives learners a framework for connecting it to what they already know, so that learning is meaningful and not rote. 'A scaffolding device' is tempting, but scaffolding is support given during a task and then withdrawn. A schema is the learner's own mental structure, and a mnemonic is a memory aid. 
- P5-Q22 -> 2.3: answer and explanation: (C)**, because Jerome Bruner advocated discovery learning (1-III); Robert Gagné set out the conditions of learning and a hierarchy of learning types (2-IV); Abraham Maslow proposed the hierarchy of needs (3-I); and Lawrence Kohlberg described the stages of moral development (4-II). Maslow and the hierarchy of needs is the anchor pair; Bruner and Gagné are often exchanged.
