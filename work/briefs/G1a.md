
## 1.1 The concept of teaching  (32 booklet questions)

### Core must teach
- S1-Q19 [single] Indoctrination implants beliefs as unquestionable and discourages critical examination. Instruction delivers structured content, training builds skills by practice, tutoring gives individual help; these allow questioning.
- S1-Q31 [single] Teaching is a purposeful, planned activity to produce learning in another person; it is not accidental and is not defined by finishing the syllabus or maintaining discipline.
- S2-Q33 [single] Training aims at skill proficiency through practice and drill; instruction imparts structured knowledge; indoctrination implants unquestioned beliefs; counselling helps with personal/academic problems.
- S3-Q15 [single] Conditioning builds a fixed stimulus-response connection by pairing or reinforcement (Pavlov, Skinner), without requiring understanding; it sits at the lowest end of the conditioning-training-instruction-teaching continuum.
- S3-Q41 [single] N. L. Gage (1963): teaching is a form of interpersonal influence aimed at changing the behaviour potential of another person; involves two persons and intention to change behaviour.
- S4-Q41 [single] Continuum: conditioning, training, instruction, teaching; each later term is wider. Training shapes skills, instruction imparts knowledge, teaching covers both plus understanding, attitudes and values.
- S4-Q46 [single] Teaching is intentional activity aimed at learning; the two are related but distinct processes; learning also occurs through experience and self-study without teaching.
- S5-Q27 [single] Instruction is systematic imparting of knowledge (possible without a teacher, via programmed text or computer); it is part of teaching, which is wider. Conditioning, indoctrination and propaganda do not aim at understanding or free judgement.
- S5-Q28 [single] Teaching brings together teacher, learner and subject matter (content); effective teaching attends to all three.  FLAG: DOUBT: tripolar process is often given as teacher-learner-curriculum (Bloom's tripolar: objectives, learning experiences, evaluation is a different triad); guide should distinguish the two tripolar notions.
- S6-Q35 [single] Teaching transmits knowledge, develops skills, inculcates values while allowing reasoning; imposing unquestionable ideology is indoctrination.
- S6-Q49 [single] Teaching as science: systematic principles of learning, motivation and evaluation that can be observed, tested and taught; as art: creative personal application.
- S7-Q50 [sequence] Conditioning links response to stimulus; training builds skill through practice; instruction imparts organised knowledge; teaching aims at understanding, reasoning and values.
- S8-Q9 [assertion-reason] Teaching is a purposeful/goal-directed activity aimed at desired changes in learners; without objectives an activity is not teaching.
- S9-Q2 [two-statement] Instruction can be delivered by programmed texts, computers, broadcasts without a teacher present; indoctrination presents beliefs as unquestionable.
- S10-Q9 [single] Morrison (1934): intimate contact of more mature and less mature personality; Gage: interpersonal influence; B.O. Smith: system of actions to induce learning; Amidon: interactive process of classroom talk.
- S11-Q25 [assertion-reason] Training builds specific skills through practice and is narrower; teaching includes skill but also aims at understanding, attitudes and critical judgement.
- S11-Q48 [multi] Teaching is a means and learning the end; teaching is judged by the learning it produces; learning is both process and product. Not all learning results from teaching, since much is learnt through experience.
- S12-Q34 [multi] Teaching behaviour can be observed, analysed and modified (interaction analysis, micro-teaching); teaching can be formal or informal; teaching is an interactive two-way process depending on feedback, not one-way.
- S13-Q37 [assertion-reason] Learning needs the learner's own attention and effort; since nobody can learn on another's behalf, arousing and sustaining motivation is a necessary function of teaching.
- S13-Q47 [two-statement] B. O. Smith: teaching is a system of actions intended to induce learning. Morrison: intimate contact between a more mature and a less mature personality. Gage: a form of interpersonal influence aimed at changing another's behaviour potential.
- S14-Q46 [assertion-reason] On the teaching continuum, activities are ranked by the role of the learner's understanding: conditioning, which relies on stimulus-response association and by-passes understanding, is at the bottom; training and instruction are higher; teaching is highest.
- S15-Q18 [assertion-reason] Teaching always involves intention to bring about learning, but learning is wider and may occur incidentally from unplanned experience and observation, so learning can occur without teaching.
- S15-Q38 [scenario] Training builds a specific skill through repeated practice to a set standard without concern for understanding; instruction imparts structured knowledge; teaching aims at understanding and judgement; conditioning establishes stimulus-response connections.
- S16-Q38 [assertion-reason] Teaching is logically triadic: someone (teacher) teaches something (content) to someone (learner). That teaching is a profession is a separate social fact and does not explain the triadic structure.
- S17-Q30 [assertion-reason] Evaluation is integral to teaching because the teacher must know whether objectives are achieved to adjust instruction. Teaching being communication is a separate characteristic.
- S17-Q42 [scenario] Instruction is organised imparting of knowledge and can be done by a book, programme or machine; teaching in the strict sense involves a person interacting with the learner. All teaching includes instruction, not all instruction is teaching.
- S18-Q30 [two-statement] Teaching fits the communication model: sender, message, channel, receiver, feedback. Noise is anything distorting the message: physical, semantic (unfamiliar terms), psychological (anxiety, prejudice).
- S19-Q11 [scenario] Training: repeated practice of a specific skill to a standard. Teaching: explanation with reasons engaging understanding and objections. Indoctrination: beliefs presented as beyond question. Conditioning: stimulus-response association without reason.
- S20-Q32 [two-statement] Indoctrination implants beliefs to be held regardless of evidence; it is marked by manner and aim, not subject matter. Any content can be taught with reasons or indoctrinated without them.

### Going Further must teach
- S12-Q29 [single] Thomas Green: training and conditioning shape behaviour; instructing and indoctrinating shape knowledge and belief. On each side the first (training, instructing) is closer to teaching because it engages intelligence; conditioning and indoctrination lie farthest.
- S14-Q4 [two-statement] Philosophers distinguish teaching in the task sense (trying; one may teach though nobody learns) from the achievement sense (succeeding; saying something was taught implies it was learnt, as selling implies buying).
- S20-Q13 [multi] Teaching is intentional and presupposes a learner; in the task sense it need not result in learning (only the achievement sense implies learning); Scheffler: teaching respects learner's independent judgement, unlike indoctrination.

## 1.2 Teaching as a process: phases, variables and operations  (23 booklet questions)

### Core must teach
- S1-Q36 [single] Philip Jackson's phases: pre-active (planning objectives, content, methods), interactive (classroom encounter), post-active (evaluation and reflection). 'Reactive phase' is not one.
- S1-Q50 [single] Teacher = independent variable; learner = dependent variable whose behaviour changes; content, methods and strategies = intervening variables.
- S3-Q10 [single] Interactive phase covers everything from entering class to lesson end (presenting, questioning, responding, reinforcing); planning = pre-active; evaluation afterwards = post-active.
- S5-Q34 [single] Post-active phase follows teaching: testing achievement of objectives, selecting testing devices, changing strategies; findings feed back into pre-active planning. 'Inactive phase' is not a phase.
- S7-Q24 [multi] Pre-active: fixing objectives, selecting and sequencing content, planning; interactive: presenting stimuli, feedback, reinforcement; post-active: choosing testing devices, evaluating outcomes.
- S8-Q44 [scenario] Jackson: pre-active = deliberate planning; interactive = spontaneous decisions while facing class; post-active = reflection and evaluation after teaching.
- S10-Q11 [single] Teaching variables: teacher = independent (manipulates process), learner = dependent (behaviour changes), content/method/environment = intervening.
- S10-Q38 [sequence] Pre-active: fixing objectives then deciding strategies; interactive: reinforcement during lesson; post-active: changing strategy in light of test results.
- S12-Q7 [two-statement] The teacher is the independent variable, the learner the dependent variable acting per the teacher's planning, and content, methods and strategies are intervening variables through which the learner's behaviour is changed.
- S12-Q46 [assertion-reason] In the pre-active (planning) phase decisions are calm and rational; in the interactive phase the teacher must respond at once to learners' cues with little time to deliberate, so decisions are spontaneous.
- S14-Q16 [single] Teacher = independent variable, learner = dependent variable, content/methods/strategies = intervening variables; the teacher selects and organises the intervening variables, through which the teacher's influence reaches the learner.
- S14-Q49 [multi] Post-active operations: defining the exact dimensions of behaviour changes achieved, selecting testing devices and techniques, and changing strategies in light of evidence. Sizing up the class (perception of size and mood) is an interactive-phase operation.
- S15-Q1 [single] Teacher = independent variable; learner = dependent variable; content, strategies and methods stand between and carry the teacher's influence, so they are intervening variables.
- S15-Q30 [multi] Pre-active: fixing objectives, deciding content and strategies. Interactive: sizing up the class, diagnosing learners, action and reaction. Post-active: evaluating outcomes, selecting tests, revising strategies, reflecting.
- S16-Q26 [scenario] Analysing a lesson already taught is post-active (evaluation, reflection); planning the next lesson is pre-active; interactive is classroom delivery. Phases feed into each other cyclically.
- S17-Q36 [two-statement] In analysis of teaching, teacher is independent variable, learner dependent, and content/methods intervening. In an experiment, the manipulated factor (method) is independent and the measured outcome (achievement) is dependent.
- S18-Q12 [multi] Post-active reflection can lead to action research; interactive decisions are steered by learners' verbal and non-verbal feedback; post-active evaluation feeds the next pre-active plan; experience shortens but does not remove planning.
- S20-Q49 [scenario] Preparing questions at home is pre-active; asking them in class to diagnose is interactive; analysing scripts and revising notes afterwards is post-active. Phase is decided by when and why an act is done.

### Going Further must teach
- S4-Q38 [single] Three functions of teaching: diagnostic (finding entry behaviour and difficulties), prescriptive (selecting content, methods, strategies), evaluative (checking achievement of objectives).
- S7-Q42 [scenario] Functions of teaching: diagnostic (finding entry behaviour and weaknesses), prescriptive (choosing content/activities to remedy), evaluative (checking outcomes).
- S9-Q36 [single] Functions of teaching: diagnostic (entry behaviour), prescriptive (choosing strategy and aids), evaluative (judging outcomes against objectives and improving teaching).
- S18-Q22 [scenario] Manipulated factor is independent; measured outcome is dependent; a moderator changes the strength of the relation; an intervening variable lies on the causal path transmitting the effect.
- S20-Q19 [assertion-reason] In experiments comparing methods, random assignment spreads extraneous learner variables (intelligence, motivation, background) evenly so that outcome differences can be attributed to the method. ## 1.3 Aims, goals, objectives and learning outcomes  (19 booklet questions)

### Core must teach
- S2-Q11 [single] An instructional objective states what the learner will be able to do after instruction in observable, assessable terms (define, explain, solve), not teacher activities, syllabus topics or lesson time.
- S2-Q13 [single] Learning is relatively permanent change in behaviour; teaching aims to produce desirable change in knowledge, skills and attitudes. Syllabus coverage, marks and order are means or by-products.
- S2-Q46 [single] Functions of teaching include transmitting knowledge, developing skills, inculcating values and socialisation, i.e., helping learners adjust to societal norms; certification and examination are system outcomes.
- S3-Q14 [single] Mager's 1962 book taught writing behavioural objectives with three parts: performance, conditions, criterion. Bloom = taxonomy; Tyler = curriculum principles; Gagné = conditions of learning.
- S4-Q49 [single] Mager's objective has performance (what learner does), conditions (circumstances/materials) and criterion (standard of acceptable performance, e.g., 8 of 10 correct).
- S5-Q19 [single] Aims are broad, long-term purposes (e.g., responsible citizens); instructional objectives are narrow, precise, achievable and testable at the end of a lesson or unit.
- S6-Q7 [single] ABCD objectives: Audience (who), Behaviour (what), Condition (circumstances), Degree (how well); extends Mager's performance, condition, criterion.
- S8-Q43 [multi] Well-written objectives: stated from learner's side, observable verbs (list, explain, solve), conditions and criterion (Mager); vague verbs like know, understand, appreciate are not measurable.
- S11-Q5 [single] In outcome-based education curriculum design begins with intended learning outcomes; content, teaching activities and assessment are then derived and aligned with them. Content-first, staff-first or time-first planning is the traditional practice OBE replaced.
- S11-Q37 [two-statement] A behavioural objective states the learner's observable performance, not the teacher's activity. Mager's three parts are performance, condition and criterion (e.g. label parts / on a given diagram / with not more than one error).
- S15-Q17 [single] Mager's three parts: performance (what the learner will do), condition (circumstances, e.g. 'given an outline map'), criterion (standard, e.g. '90 per cent accuracy').
- S19-Q30 [assertion-reason] Teaching in higher education aims not only at transmitting knowledge but at developing critical thinking, inquiry, judgement, research orientation, values and capacity to create new knowledge.
- S20-Q9 [assertion-reason] Behavioural objectives specify observable learner performance (Mager), which makes evaluation easier; statements of teacher activities are not objectives.

### Going Further must teach
- S9-Q6 [single] RCEM = Regional College of Education Mysore approach: objectives in categories knowledge, understanding, application, creativity, stated through specified mental processes instead of action verbs.  FLAG: DOUBT: explanation says RCEM specifies seventeen mental processes; sources vary on the count - verify before the guide states a number.
- S13-Q11 [single] In OBE, course outcomes state what students can do at the end of a course; programme outcomes and programme-specific outcomes state attainments at graduation; programme educational objectives describe what graduates achieve a few years into their careers.
- S14-Q27 [single] Gronlund recommended stating a general instructional objective (e.g. understands principles) clarified by a sample of specific learning outcomes in behavioural terms. The single-behaviour format with condition and criterion is Mager's.
- S14-Q33 [assertion-reason] In OBE, assessment is aligned with stated outcomes because its purpose is to determine whether intended outcomes have been attained; constructive alignment (Biggs) links outcomes, teaching activities and assessment.
- S17-Q46 [single] Terminal objective: what the learner can do at the end. Enabling objectives: intermediate component skills found by task analysis. Expressive objectives (Eisner) describe an encounter without specifying outcome.
- S19-Q9 [scenario] John Biggs's constructive alignment requires teaching activities and assessment tasks to call for the same performance as the intended learning outcome; mismatch is a failure of alignment, not of reliability or predictive validity.

## 1.4 Taxonomies of educational objectives  (70 booklet questions)

### Core must teach
- S1-Q6 [single] Revised taxonomy order: Remember, Understand, Apply, Analyse, Evaluate, Create; Create is highest. In the 1956 original, Evaluation was highest with Synthesis below it.
- S1-Q12 [single] Krathwohl, Bloom and Masia published the affective taxonomy in 1964 (Receiving to Characterisation). Simpson and Dave are psychomotor taxonomists; Gagné is known for conditions of learning.
- S1-Q22 [single] Physical skills needing mind-muscle coordination, like handling apparatus, are psychomotor. Cognitive is thinking, affective is attitudes/values, conative is will or striving.
- S1-Q24 [single] Cognitive domain covers knowing and thinking from recall to judgement; attitudes/feelings/values are affective; motor skills are psychomotor.
- S1-Q47 [single] Original order: Knowledge, Comprehension, Application, Analysis, Synthesis, Evaluation; Knowledge (recall) is lowest, renamed Remember in 2001.
- S2-Q1 [single] Krathwohl's affective levels in order: Receiving, Responding, Valuing, Organisation, Characterisation; Receiving (willingness to attend) is lowest.
- S2-Q27 [single] Revised taxonomy turned nouns into verbs: Knowledge became Remember, Comprehension became Understand, etc.; Synthesis became Create and moved above Evaluate. Recall and recognise are processes under Remember, not level names.
- S2-Q39 [single] Original order: Knowledge, Comprehension, Application, Analysis, Synthesis, Evaluation; Comprehension means grasping meaning (explain, summarise).
- S3-Q11 [single] Action verbs: Remember (define, list, name, state, recall); Apply (solve, use); Analyse (compare, differentiate); Create (design, compose).
- S3-Q23 [single] Affective levels: Receiving, Responding, Valuing, Organisation, Characterisation by a value or value complex; at Characterisation values form a consistent philosophy governing behaviour.
- S3-Q34 [single] Synthesis (fifth original level) combines elements into a new pattern or structure; Analysis breaks a whole into parts; Application uses learning in new situations; Comprehension grasps meaning.
- S3-Q39 [single] Original taxonomy used nouns (Knowledge, Comprehension, Application...); the 2001 revision uses verbs (Remember, Understand, Apply, Analyse, Evaluate, Create).
- S4-Q1 [single] Analysis (fourth original level) separates a whole into elements and finds relations (e.g., facts vs opinions); Synthesis combines parts into a new whole; Evaluation judges worth against criteria; Comprehension grasps meaning.
- S4-Q3 [single] Receiving = merely attending; Responding = reacting actively and willingly (answering, participating, volunteering); Valuing = attaching worth and showing commitment; Organisation = building a value system.
- S4-Q35 [sequence] Revised levels in order: Remember, Understand, Apply, Analyse, Evaluate, Create.
- S5-Q3 [scenario] Application is using a learnt rule or method in a new concrete situation; stating it is Knowledge, explaining it is Comprehension, judging worth is Evaluation.
- S5-Q15 [single] Mapping: Knowledge to Remember, Comprehension to Understand, Application to Apply, Analysis to Analyse; Synthesis became Create and moved to top above Evaluate.
- S5-Q26 [scenario] Valuing means attaching worth and acting on it consistently; Receiving = attention; Responding = participation. Imitation is a psychomotor (Dave) level, not affective.
- S6-Q12 [single] Create verbs: design, construct, compose, formulate, plan; Remember: list, define; Understand: explain, summarise; Apply: solve, demonstrate.
- S6-Q14 [single] 1956 order: Knowledge, Comprehension, Application, Analysis, Synthesis, Evaluation; 2001 revision puts Create (renamed Synthesis) above Evaluate.
- S6-Q37 [single] Revised order: Remember, Understand, Apply, Analyse, Evaluate, Create; Evaluate is fifth, below Create.
- S7-Q1 [scenario] Analyse = breaking material into parts and seeing relations; processes differentiating, organising, attributing; Evaluate needs judgement against criteria; Understand = explaining; Create = producing new.
- S7-Q6 [single] Simpson: perception, set, guided response, mechanism, complex overt response, adaptation, origination; Dave begins with imitation; Harrow with reflex movements; Receiving is affective.
- S7-Q14 [scenario] Bloom 1956 Comprehension has three forms: translation, interpretation, extrapolation; changing words to a graph is translation, not Application or Analysis.
- S7-Q23 [match] Summarise = Understand; Differentiate = Analyse; Justify = Evaluate; Compose = Create.
- S7-Q31 [sequence] Affective order: Receiving, Responding, Valuing, Organisation, Characterisation (by a value complex); principle of internalisation.
- S8-Q31 [scenario] Evaluate = judgements based on criteria (checking, critiquing); Create = producing new; Analyse = breaking into parts; Apply = executing procedures.
- S8-Q47 [single] Organising principles: cognitive - complexity; affective - internalisation; psychomotor - coordination.
- S9-Q15 [multi] Affective = feelings, attitudes, values (appreciates, shows respect); cognitive = intellectual (solves equations); psychomotor = manual skill (operates microscope).
- S9-Q32 [single] Lower-order: Knowledge, Comprehension, Application; higher-order: Analysis, Synthesis, Evaluation.
- S9-Q39 [scenario] Organisation: bringing values together, resolving conflicts, building consistent value system; Valuing concerns single value; Characterisation = value system controls behaviour consistently over time.
- S9-Q47 [scenario] Revised taxonomy knowledge dimension: factual (terms, details), conceptual (classifications, principles, theories), procedural (methods, techniques, algorithms), metacognitive (awareness of own thinking).
- S10-Q25 [single] Krathwohl's affective levels ordered by internalisation (Receiving to Characterisation); cognitive by complexity; psychomotor by coordination.
- S10-Q30 [single] Anderson and Krathwohl taxonomy table: knowledge dimension (factual, conceptual, procedural, metacognitive) x cognitive process dimension (Remember to Create).
- S10-Q45 [multi] Bloom's cognitive levels form a cumulative hierarchy; Handbook I (1956) cognitive; Handbook II (1964, Krathwohl, Bloom, Masia) affective.
- S11-Q23 [match] Listing features is Remember; using a learnt procedure on a new case is Apply; distinguishing categories (differentiating) is Analyse; drafting something new is Create.
- S12-Q17 [assertion-reason] Affective outcomes are hard to evaluate because attitudes and values are internal, develop slowly and can be faked. Krathwohl's affective taxonomy has five levels (receiving, responding, valuing, organisation, characterisation), but this does not explain the difficulty.
- S12-Q21 [multi] In the revised taxonomy Create is the highest process (Synthesis renamed and moved up); the knowledge dimension has four categories (factual, conceptual, procedural, metacognitive); Understand includes interpreting, exemplifying, classifying, summarising, inferring, comparing, explaining.
- S12-Q27 [single] Original Bloom's Synthesis includes producing a unique communication, a plan, or a set of abstract relations (e.g. a hypothesis to account for data); it corresponds to Create in the revised taxonomy. Analysis only breaks material into elements; Evaluation judges against criteria.
- S13-Q28 [assertion-reason] In Bloom's 1956 taxonomy Evaluation is at the top above Synthesis; in the 2001 revision Create (former Synthesis) is placed above Evaluate. Evaluation means judging value using internal evidence or external criteria.
- S13-Q43 [multi] Krathwohl, Bloom and Masia (1964) affective levels: receiving (awareness, willingness to receive, selected attention), responding, valuing, organisation, characterisation (highest).
- S14-Q18 [single] Revised taxonomy order: Remember, Understand, Apply, Analyse, Evaluate, Create. Formulating a proposal = Create (highest); critiquing against criteria = Evaluate; distinguishing = Analyse; computing by known procedure = Apply.
- S15-Q14 [match] Receiving = attending; Responding = active participation (e.g. volunteering comment); Valuing = attaching worth and commitment; Organisation = building a value system; Characterisation = a value system consistently governing behaviour.
- S15-Q35 [single] In the 2001 revision, Evaluation became Evaluate at level five and Synthesis, renamed Create, moved to the top; categories became verbs and Knowledge became Remember.
- S15-Q50 [single] The knowledge dimension has factual, conceptual, procedural and metacognitive knowledge; metacognitive knowledge (added in the revision) is knowledge of cognition in general and of one's own cognition, including strategies and self-knowledge.
- S17-Q24 [scenario] Justifying a choice against criteria is the cognitive process Evaluate; Valuing is affective, concerning attitude and commitment.
- S19-Q16 [assertion-reason] Bloom 1956 order: Knowledge, Comprehension, Application, Analysis, Synthesis, Evaluation, simple to complex, each building on those below; Comprehension is prerequisite to Application.
- S20-Q33 [sequence] Define = Remember; explain why = Understand; compute using a known procedure = Apply; design = Create. Ascending: Remember, Understand, Apply, Analyse, Evaluate, Create.

## 1.5 Levels of teaching: memory, understanding and reflective  (76 booklet questions)

### Core must teach
- S1-Q3 [single] Understanding level (Morrison) goes beyond rote memory to seeing relationships among facts and generalisations so learners can explain and apply them. Rote recall is memory level; independent problem-solving is reflective level.
- S1-Q32 [single] Three levels in rising order: memory, understanding, reflective; reflective is highest and includes the lower two. 'Autonomous development level' is not one of the standard levels.
- S1-Q44 [single] Herbart is the exponent of memory-level teaching; Morrison of understanding level; Hunt of reflective level. Bloom is known for the taxonomy, not a level.
- S1-Q48 [single] Memory-level teaching focuses on committing facts to memory and reproducing them; teacher active, learner passive; lowest of the three levels (memory, understanding, reflective).
- S2-Q8 [single] Morrison's understanding-level model has five steps: exploration, presentation, assimilation, organisation, recitation; aim is mastery of units. Thorndike and Skinner are learning theorists; Dale proposed the Cone of Experience.
- S2-Q15 [sequence] Levels from lowest to highest are memory, understanding, reflective; each higher level presupposes the lower ones.
- S2-Q36 [single] Reflective-level teaching is problem-centred: teacher creates a problematic situation; learners examine critically, form and test hypotheses, reach conclusions. Memorising/reciting belong to memory level.
- S2-Q45 [single] Memory-level teaching is teacher-dominated; learner receives, retains, reproduces. Active inquiry and independent criticism belong to the reflective level.
- S3-Q2 [single] Teacher control is greatest at memory level, less at understanding level, least at reflective level where learners investigate and the teacher guides.
- S3-Q6 [single] Memory-level teaching uses telling, drill, repetition, recitation and review; debate, projects, seminars require understanding/reflection and belong to higher levels.
- S3-Q8 [single] Understanding shows when learners restate ideas, give examples, see relationships and apply principles in familiar situations. Exact repetition is memory level; independent discovery is reflective level.
- S3-Q18 [single] Reflective-level teaching model is linked with Hunt and discussed by Bigge; problem-centred, aiming at critical independent thinking. Herbart = memory level; Pavlov and Thorndike are learning theorists.  FLAG: DOUBT: attribution variously given as Hunt, Bigge, or Bigge and Hunt; guide should present both names.
- S4-Q10 [single] Memory-level evaluation uses recall and recognition (objective) items; understanding level needs explanation/application items; reflective level uses essay-type and open-ended tasks.
- S4-Q13 [single] Herbartian steps (memory level): preparation, presentation, comparison/association, generalisation, application. Exploration, assimilation, organisation, recitation (with presentation) are Morrison's understanding-level steps.
- S4-Q24 [single] Morrison's five steps: exploration (finding what learners already know), presentation, assimilation, organisation, recitation.
- S4-Q37 [single] At reflective level learners face problems, question assumptions, weigh evidence and produce solutions, developing critical and creative thinking and independence; memorisation, imitation and listening suffice only for lower levels.
- S5-Q18 [single] Understanding needs facts, and reflection needs facts and understanding, so memory level is the base for understanding and reflective levels. 'Insight level' is not a standard level.
- S5-Q23 [single] Rote-memorised material has few links, fades quickly and cannot be applied; memory level also leaves learners passive. Criticism and lengthy investigation belong to the reflective level.

## Booklet 0 (diagnostic) questions the guide must also teach
- P1-Q6 -> 1.5: answer and explanation: (D)**, because the reflective level, the highest of the three levels of teaching, is linked with Hunt; it is problem-centred and asks learners to examine and test ideas critically. The memory level is associated with Herbart and the understanding level with Morrison. Bloom is known for the taxonomy of educational objectives, not for a level of teaching. 
- P1-Q38 -> 1.4: answer and explanation: (B)**, because judging the merits of alternatives against criteria and justifying the choice is 'Evaluating' in the revised Bloom's taxonomy. 'Analysing' would stop at breaking each design into its parts and examining how they relate. 'Applying' means using a procedure in a new situation, and 'Understanding' means explaining ideas in one's own words. 
- P3-Q30 -> 1.4: answer and explanation: (C)**, because the outcome is about valuing and acting consistently on a value, which belongs to the affective domain. Krathwohl's affective taxonomy runs from receiving and responding to valuing, organisation and characterisation by a value. 'Cognitive' is tempting because research sounds like an intellectual matter, but the verb that carries the outcome is 'value', not 'know' or 'analyse'.
