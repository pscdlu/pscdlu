
## 4.1 Approach, method, strategy and technique; teacher-centred and learner-centred methods  (10 booklet questions)

### Core must teach
- S1-Q46 [single] In learner-centred teaching students are active and the teacher arranges conditions, guides and supports as facilitator; teacher as transmitter of information is typical of teacher-centred approach.
- S3-Q46 [single] In group discussion learners do most talking and thinking with teacher guidance (learner-centred); lecture, dictation and teacher demonstration are teacher-centred.
- S4-Q17 [single] Inductive method: learners examine particular examples and derive the general rule (suits discovery). Deductive method states rule/definition first then applies to examples (saves time).
- S6-Q38 [single] Teacher-centred: teacher controls content and pace, learners listen and take notes; discovering, joint planning and peer tutoring are learner-centred roles.
- S8-Q32 [multi] Learner-centred methods make the learner the chief actor (project, problem-solving, discussion); dictation is teacher-controlled.
- S10-Q2 [single] Learner-centred approach: learner constructs knowledge through activity, teacher designs and guides; continuous assessment and cooperation are learner-centred features.
- S11-Q27 [match] Lecture is large-group and teacher-centred; tutorial is small-group and remedial with a tutor; programmed instruction is individualised and self-paced; brainstorming is group generation of ideas.
- S13-Q33 [assertion-reason] In learner-centred methods learners are active: what they work out, discuss and apply is processed more deeply and retained longer. Passive learners and an active teacher characterise teacher-centred methods.
- S17-Q14 [multi] Learner-centred teaching changes the teacher's role to designer and guide but does not make it unimportant, and uses formative assessment extensively. Teacher-centred exposition suits introducing unfamiliar content; choice depends on objectives, learners and content.
- S19-Q43 [single] NEP 2020 calls for experiential, holistic, integrated, inquiry-driven, discovery-oriented, learner-centred, discussion-based and flexible pedagogy with less rote learning.

## 4.2 Teacher-centred methods: lecture, demonstration and programmed instruction  (24 booklet questions)

### Core must teach
- S1-Q11 [single] In a lecture the teacher controls content and pace while learners listen, so it is teacher-centred; economical for large classes but little learner activity.
- S2-Q34 [single] In demonstration the teacher shows step by step how something is done while explaining (lab experiment, instrument use), so it suits practical skills and procedures.
- S2-Q50 [single] Programmed instruction breaks content into small frames, requires active response, gives immediate confirmation (reinforcement) and allows self-pacing; it applies Skinner's operant conditioning.
- S3-Q37 [single] Lecture's main advantage is economy: much organised content to a large class quickly; weaknesses are little activity, little interaction and no adjustment to individual pace.
- S4-Q28 [single] Lecture communication is mostly one-way so learners listen passively with little questioning or application; attention falls after 15-20 minutes. Syllabus coverage, handling large classes and introducing topics are strengths.
- S5-Q10 [single] Lecture attention drops after about 15 minutes; breaking it with questions, examples, short activities and visual aids restores attention and makes it two-way; speaking fast, reading from the book or avoiding eye contact harm it.
- S5-Q48 [single] A programme is a sequence of frames, each giving small information, requiring a response, with immediate check; principles: small steps, active responding, immediate confirmation, self-pacing.
- S6-Q24 [single] Lecture-cum-demonstration: teacher explains a principle while demonstrating with apparatus/models; widely used in science; more concrete than pure lecture.
- S7-Q5 [multi] Lecture suits large classes and can present material not in textbooks; it does not develop psychomotor skills and is largely one-way, so no feedback from every learner.
- S7-Q43 [scenario] Programmed instruction principles: small steps, active responding, immediate confirmation (feedback), self-pacing, student testing (Skinner).
- S8-Q27 [single] Demonstration needs visibility and audibility, rehearsal, step-by-step moderate pace, simple apparatus and questioning during it.
- S9-Q44 [assertion-reason] Lecture economy: high learner-teacher ratio and much content per hour, so low cost and time per student.
- S10-Q13 [single] Attention is high at the start of a lecture and is commonly said to decline after about 15-20 minutes; lectures should be segmented with questions, tasks or change of medium.  FLAG: DOUBT: the 15-20 minute attention-span claim is widely taught but its research basis is contested (e.g. Wilson & Korn 2007; Bradbury 2016); guide may present it as conventional advice.
- S10-Q21 [single] Skinner's linear programme: small steps so responses are almost always correct and reinforced; Crowder's branching programme uses errors for diagnosis.
- S12-Q38 [assertion-reason] The lecture is weak for psychomotor skills because skills need learner practice and lecture learners only listen; its suitability for large groups is a separate feature, not the cause.
- S14-Q30 [assertion-reason] In linear programming all learners take the same path, only pace varies; in branching programming the learner's response routes him to remedial frames or ahead, so both path and pace are individualised.
- S14-Q40 [single] A demonstration secures learning when students are asked to predict, observe and explain each step, engaging thinking and exposing misconceptions; announcing conclusions first or performing silently leaves them passive, and all must be able to see.
- S15-Q7 [assertion-reason] The lecture is economical, letting one teacher present an organised overview to many students quickly, useful for introducing topics; its weakness is one-way communication and comparatively passive learners.
- S15-Q32 [scenario] In Crowder's branching (intrinsic) programming each frame ends with a multiple-choice question and wrong choices lead to remedial frames before returning to the main sequence. Skinner's linear programming takes all learners through the same small steps with minimal errors.
- S16-Q34 [multi] Demonstration is more concrete than lecture, but the teacher performs while learners watch, so it gives no hands-on practice. Lecture efficiently transmits information but is not suited to developing skills.
- S16-Q36 [single] Crowder's branching: larger frames, multiple-choice responses, wrong choices lead to remedial frames (errors used diagnostically). Skinner's linear: small steps, constructed responses, near-errorless learning.
- S18-Q20 [single] In communication terms a lecture has sender, message, channel and receivers, but largely lacks feedback; questions, pauses and short tasks restore the feedback loop.
- S20-Q18 [assertion-reason] Programmed instruction principles include small steps, active responding, immediate feedback (knowledge of results) and self-pacing; a pace fixed for the whole class marks conventional teaching.
- S20-Q48 [scenario] Lecture suits giving an overview to a large group quickly; manipulative skills require demonstration followed by individual laboratory practice with feedback.

## 4.3 Group and interactive methods  (41 booklet questions)

### Core must teach
- S1-Q4 [single] A tutorial is a small-group session where a tutor clears doubts and gives personal feedback, supplementing lectures. Lecture addresses a large group; symposium and conference involve formal presentations by several speakers.
- S1-Q17 [single] Brainstorming, introduced by Alex Osborn, asks a group to produce as many ideas as possible, deferring criticism/evaluation. It is not for memorising, testing or revising.
- S1-Q33 [single] In a panel discussion a few experts discuss among themselves before an audience, guided by a moderator. Tutorial = small-group guidance; workshop = hands-on practice; demonstration = showing how something is done.
- S2-Q10 [single] In a seminar a student or scholar presents a prepared paper, then the group discusses and questions it under a chairperson. Supervised skill practice describes a workshop.
- S2-Q37 [single] Cooperative learning places students in small mixed groups with individual and group responsibility for a shared goal, developing academic and social skills; opposite of individual competition or solitary work.
- S3-Q13 [single] A workshop is a working session where participants learn by doing, practising a skill or producing something with guidance from resource persons. Paper reading = seminar; expert debate = panel/symposium.
- S3-Q44 [single] Osborn's rules: defer judgement, welcome unusual ideas, aim at quantity, build on others' ideas; evaluation is a separate later stage.
- S4-Q21 [single] A symposium is a series of short prepared talks by different speakers on aspects of one theme, followed by audience questions. Distinguish from lecture, role play and micro-teaching.
- S5-Q42 [single] Alex F. Osborn, advertising executive, described brainstorming in Applied Imagination. De Bono = lateral thinking/six hats; Guilford = structure of intellect/divergent thinking; Lyman = Think-Pair-Share.
- S5-Q50 [single] The moderator introduces topic and panel, puts questions, ensures fair turns, prevents digression and sums up; a rapporteur records proceedings and reports; an invigilator supervises exams.
- S6-Q47 [single] Buzz session: large class split into groups of about 4-6 who discuss briefly and report back; brings participation into large classes.
- S7-Q26 [match] Seminar: paper presented then discussed in depth; symposium: successive prepared talks on aspects of a theme; panel: moderated informal conversation of experts before audience; workshop: hands-on production under resource persons.
- S7-Q34 [single] Aronson's jigsaw: home groups, each member gets one part, expert groups study same part, return to teach home group; positive interdependence.
- S9-Q9 [single] Team teaching: teachers pool strengths and share planning, teaching and evaluation of the same students; peer tutoring is students teaching each other.
- S9-Q45 [scenario] Seminar: one paper presented and examined in depth (discussant, chairperson); symposium: several speakers on aspects of theme; panel: expert conversation; workshop: practical work.
- S10-Q3 [single] Debate: motion, two opposing sides, fixed speaker order, time limits; panel exchanges views without fixed sides; symposium presents aspects of theme; colloquium is an academic discussion meeting.
- S11-Q10 [scenario] In a panel discussion a few experts converse informally without prepared speeches under a moderator, then take audience questions. In a symposium each speaker delivers a prepared talk on one aspect in turn, without conversing.
- S11-Q13 [two-statement] Osborn's brainstorming rules: defer judgement/criticism until generation is over, aim for quantity of ideas, welcome unusual ideas, and combine/build on others' ideas. Evaluation and quality sorting come afterwards.
- S11-Q41 [scenario] Team teaching means a group of teachers jointly plan, teach and evaluate the same students, typically combining large-group lectures, small-group discussion and independent study. A tutorial system merely supplements one teacher's lectures; peer tutoring uses students as tutors.
- S12-Q8 [scenario] Tutorials deal with individual and small-group difficulties lectures cannot reach; diagnosing a shared misconception and giving targeted exercises is remedial. Summative grades, placement assigns position, prognostic predicts future performance.
- S12-Q47 [multi] A seminar has a chairperson, paper presenter(s) and participants; a symposium has prepared talks and normally ends with audience questions; a workshop stresses hands-on practical work; a conference covers a broad theme through several sessions.
- S14-Q6 [sequence] A seminar session: chairperson introduces the theme and speaker, the paper is presented, participants discuss and question, and the chairperson sums up and closes.
- S14-Q50 [multi] Team teaching uses each teacher's special competence and requires joint planning, teaching and evaluation; it demands more coordination than individual teaching, which is its main practical difficulty.
- S15-Q39 [scenario] In a symposium several speakers present prepared statements on different aspects of one theme in turn, without conversing, followed by audience questions; in a panel discussion members converse informally under a moderator.
- S16-Q5 [scenario] A workshop is defined by participants' practical work yielding a product, with short expert input. Seminars and symposia centre on presenting and discussing papers; a conference is a larger meeting for exchange.
- S18-Q29 [scenario] Symposium: several experts give prepared statements on different aspects one after another. Panel discussion: experts converse spontaneously under a moderator before an audience. Seminar centres on papers; workshop on practical product.
- S19-Q13 [assertion-reason] A conference is a large gathering on a broad theme with plenary lectures and parallel sessions where papers are presented; a seminar is smaller and narrower; both include paper presentation.
- S19-Q22 [scenario] Workshop: skill training producing a product. Symposium: successive expert presentations on aspects of a theme before an audience. Seminar: in-depth discussion and critique of a paper.
- S20-Q22 [two-statement] Peer tutoring benefits the tutor too, since explaining deepens understanding; tutorials supplement lectures by addressing individual difficulties, not replace them.
- S20-Q31 [assertion-reason] A symposium's many-sided view comes from several speakers each treating a different aspect; a closing question session is customary but not the source of breadth.

### Going Further must teach
- S4-Q43 [single] Think-Pair-Share, devised by Frank Lyman: think alone, discuss with a partner, share with class; a cooperative learning technique enabling participation in large classes.
- S8-Q49 [single] Rapporteur records and reports proceedings; convener organises; chairperson/moderator conducts; discussant critiques a paper.
- S9-Q26 [single] Johnson and Johnson essentials: positive interdependence, individual accountability, face-to-face (promotive) interaction, social skills, group processing.
- S13-Q10 [scenario] Fishbowl: an inner circle discusses while an outer circle observes content and process and later comments, possibly swapping places. Buzz groups are brief simultaneous small-group discussions; panel is experts under a moderator; round robin gives each member a turn.
- S13-Q34 [scenario] STAD (Slavin): teaching, mixed-team study, individual quizzes, team recognition based on improvement points over each member's past average. Jigsaw divides content among members who teach one another; Think-Pair-Share is individual thought, pair discussion, sharing; Group Investigation (Thelen/Sharan) has groups inquire into subtopics.
- S14-Q43 [scenario] Brainwriting generates ideas silently in writing (e.g. on cards) before discussion, so quiet members contribute equally; it keeps deferred judgement, then ideas are pooled and discussed. Buzz sessions are oral small-group talk; fishbowl has inner/outer circles.
- S17-Q22 [scenario] Supervision tutorial: tutor with one or two students on their written work (Oxbridge). Group tutorial: small group clearing common difficulties after lectures. Practical tutorial: skills in lab or workshop.
- S17-Q34 [scenario] Negative interdependence (competition): one succeeds only if others fail. Positive interdependence (cooperation): each succeeds only if others succeed. No interdependence (individualistic): results independent.
- S17-Q50 [scenario] A forum is open discussion where the whole audience asks questions and comments, usually after a lecture, panel or symposium. In panel discussion and symposium, a few selected persons on the platform do the main talking.
- S18-Q39 [single] J. Lloyd Trump plan: about 40 per cent large-group instruction, 20 per cent small-group discussion, 40 per cent independent study; teachers work as a team by strengths.
- S19-Q20 [single] Slavin found cooperative learning raises achievement when there are group goals/rewards and individual accountability (team success depends on each member's learning); group-only assessment invites free riding.

## 4.4 Learner-centred and activity methods  (38 booklet questions)

### Core must teach
- S1-Q2 [single] Kilpatrick set out the project method in 1918 on Dewey's learning by doing; steps purposing, planning, executing, evaluating. Montessori and Froebel are early-childhood educators; Helen Parkhurst devised the Dalton Plan.
- S2-Q25 [single] The project method has students carry out a purposeful real-life task from planning to completion, reflecting Dewey's pragmatism and learning by doing; it replaces rote and passive listening.
- S2-Q42 [single] In role play learners take the parts of people in a real or imagined situation and act it out unscripted, followed by discussion; builds empathy and social skills.
- S3-Q16 [single] The case method was developed at Harvard, first in law, then from the 1920s at Harvard Business School, where students analyse real business situations and propose decisions.
- S3-Q50 [sequence] Problem-solving steps: recognise/define problem, collect data, form hypotheses, test them, draw conclusion.
- S4-Q19 [sequence] Kilpatrick's four steps in order: purposing (choose a purposeful project), planning, executing, evaluating/judging against the original purpose.
- S4-Q26 [single] Simulation lets learners practise under conditions resembling reality (mock court, flight simulator, simulated teaching) where mistakes carry no real cost. Working in a real workplace is apprenticeship/internship, not simulation.
- S5-Q33 [single] A case is an account of a real or realistic problem; students study facts, identify issues, discuss alternatives and defend a decision, linking theory and practice.
- S5-Q40 [single] H. E. Armstrong advocated the heuristic method (Greek 'I find out') for science: pupils as discoverers with minimal telling. Kilpatrick = project method; Parkhurst = Dalton Plan; Montessori = early childhood method.
- S6-Q1 [single] Role play: learners act as another person to experience their situation and feelings; it builds empathy, attitudes and interpersonal skills, not recall, calculation or drawing.
- S6-Q16 [single] Bloom's mastery learning sets a fixed standard per unit (e.g. 80%) that every learner must reach, checked by criterion-referenced (formative) tests followed by correctives.
- S6-Q22 [single] Problem-solving follows scientific method steps (define problem, gather data, hypothesise, test, conclude) and develops reasoning and scientific attitude.
- S7-Q8 [scenario] Project method: purposeful real-life task with planning, execution, evaluation and tangible product; case study analyses an already written case; demonstration and tutorial differ.
- S7-Q39 [single] Role play enactment provides experience; learning comes from discussion/debriefing by players and observers linking it to objectives.
- S8-Q15 [scenario] Deductive: general rule first then particular cases (quick, practice, less discovery); inductive: from examples to rule; heuristic: learner discovers; analytic: unknown to known.
- S8-Q23 [single] Fred S. Keller's PSI: self-paced units from written material, unit mastery shown by test with student proctor, lectures only to motivate; Skinner = linear programming; Crowder = branching; Bloom = mastery learning.  FLAG: DOUBT: outline lists personalised system of instruction in 4.2 but Keller Plan in 4.4; guide should treat PSI and Keller Plan in one place.
- S8-Q45 [assertion-reason] Project method (W.H. Kilpatrick, Dewey's pragmatism) is time-consuming and gives incidental, unsystematic knowledge, so hard as sole method; propounder is unrelated reason.
- S9-Q8 [single] Bloom's mastery learning: unit formative tests (ungraded) reveal unmastered objectives; correctives and second test follow; grading by later summative test.
- S10-Q22 [two-statement] Inductive method is slow (many examples before rule) but promotes discovery; deductive is quick (rule given) and suits application, practice, revision.
- S11-Q30 [assertion-reason] The project method (Kilpatrick) centres on a purposeful real-life activity that draws on several subjects together, so it promotes correlation; it does not follow a timetable of separate fixed subject periods.
- S13-Q18 [assertion-reason] H. E. Armstrong's heuristic method places the learner in the position of a discoverer, finding things out by own observation and experiment; the teacher sets problems and guides but tells as little as possible.
- S15-Q3 [sequence] Dewey's steps (How We Think): felt difficulty; its location and definition; suggestion of possible solutions; reasoning out implications of each; testing by observation or experiment leading to acceptance or rejection. The problem-solving method follows this order.
- S15-Q15 [match] Helen Parkhurst: Dalton Plan (individual assignments/contracts). H. E. Armstrong: heuristic method. B. F. Skinner: linear programmed instruction. Alex Osborn: brainstorming.
- S16-Q11 [two-statement] A case describes a real or realistic situation for analysis and decision. Role play gives players a situation and role to improvise; a memorised script makes it dramatisation.
- S17-Q31 [assertion-reason] Both involve purposeful learner activity, but problem-solving is chiefly mental and ends in a solution, while a project is carried out in a real-life setting and ends in a tangible product.
- S18-Q17 [multi] PBL presents an ill-structured problem before content is taught; the tutor acts as facilitator; a physical product is characteristic of the project method, not PBL.
- S18-Q33 [assertion-reason] Induction moves from limited particular instances to a general rule; the conclusion goes beyond the evidence, so it is probable, whereas valid deduction yields certainty.
- S19-Q17 [two-statement] Simulation is used mainly for skills, decisions and attitudes costly or risky to practise in reality (flying, surgery, teaching); debriefing afterwards analyses the experience and turns it into learning.
- S19-Q28 [scenario] Deduction applies a general premise to a particular case; with true premises and valid form the conclusion is certain. Induction goes from observed cases to a general law and is probable; abduction infers best explanation; analogy argues from resemblance.

### Going Further must teach
- S9-Q48 [single] Kilpatrick's four project types: producer/constructive (make something), consumer/aesthetic (enjoyment, appreciation), problem (intellectual question), drill (mastering skill/knowledge).
- S10-Q39 [single] PBL developed at McMaster University medical school, Canada, late 1960s (Howard Barrows); case method linked with Harvard; micro-teaching with Stanford.
- S11-Q21 [scenario] The in-basket (in-tray) exercise is a simulation in which a trainee handles realistic letters and memos under time pressure, then decisions are discussed. Sociodrama enacts a group social problem; role reversal has two persons exchange roles; buzz sessions are small quick group discussions.
- S12-Q4 [sequence] Polya (How to Solve It): understand the problem, devise a plan, carry out the plan, look back (check and reflect).
- S12-Q19 [match] Skinner's linear programming: all learners follow the same small steps. Crowder's branching: response decides next frame. Gilbert's mathetics: retrogressive (backward) chaining, final step mastered first. Keller's PSI: self-paced units each passed with a student proctor.
- S13-Q16 [scenario] J. L. Moreno distinguished psychodrama (an individual's private problems, therapeutic) from sociodrama (problems shared by a group or community). Classroom role play on social issues like dowry is sociodrama.
- S14-Q38 [single] Stevenson: 'a problematic act carried to completion in its natural setting'. Kilpatrick: 'a whole-hearted purposeful activity proceeding in a social environment'. Ballard: 'a bit of real life imported into the school'. Kilpatrick, Dewey's follower, developed the project method.
- S17-Q29 [scenario] In the incident method (Paul Pigors) learners get a brief incident and must obtain remaining facts by questioning before analysis; in an ordinary case study full facts are given. In-basket method simulates handling a manager's papers.
- S20-Q8 [scenario] Action research (Kurt Lewin) follows a spiral of plan, act, observe, reflect to improve practice in one's own setting; ex post facto studies effects after the event without intervention; experiments use control groups and aim at generalisation. ## 4.5 Off-line and on-line teaching; blended learning and the flipped classroom  (21 booklet questions)

### Core must teach
- S1-Q28 [single] In a flipped classroom students study new material before class via videos or readings; class time is used for discussion, problem-solving and application.
- S2-Q9 [single] Blended (hybrid) learning is a planned mix of classroom teaching and online activities, giving online flexibility and in-class interaction.
- S3-Q35 [single] Since content is studied at home, flipped class time is used for clearing doubts, discussion, problem-solving and application; the lecture moves out in recorded form.
- S5-Q22 [single] Hybrid/blended learning combines classroom and online learning in one course. Distance learning separates teacher and learner; programmed learning uses frames; mastery learning requires mastery of each unit before the next.
- S5-Q46 [single] Synchronous learning happens live for all (live video class, webinar) with immediate interaction; asynchronous uses recorded lectures, forums and assignments at learners' convenience.
- S7-Q3 [single] Flipped classroom: first exposure (remember/understand) through videos/readings at home; applying, analysing, evaluating, creating in class with teacher and peers.
- S8-Q20 [scenario] Asynchronous: participants contribute at different times (forums, e-mail, recorded lectures), allowing reflection; synchronous requires simultaneous presence (live class).
- S10-Q20 [single] Blended learning keeps classroom social presence, immediate feedback and practical work and adds online flexibility and self-pacing; still needs campus, devices and careful planning.
- S10-Q49 [match] Synchronous: live video class, webinar, instant chat; asynchronous: discussion boards, e-mail, recorded lectures.
- S11-Q28 [assertion-reason] Synchronous learning requires participants online at the same time (live classes, webinars). Asynchronous learning frees learners from a common timetable, using recorded lectures, forums and email at each learner's convenience.
- S12-Q37 [assertion-reason] In a flipped classroom learners first meet new content outside class through videos or readings, freeing class time for application, discussion and higher-order activities. In-class lecture presentation describes the conventional classroom.
- S15-Q41 [multi] Recorded lectures at one's own time are asynchronous; live webinars are synchronous; blended learning combines face-to-face and online. cMOOCs are connectivist and learner-networked; xMOOCs are structured, instructor-led video-and-quiz courses.
- S16-Q3 [assertion-reason] Synchronous learning happens at the same time in real time (e.g. live webinar); freedom from a common time defines asynchronous learning.
- S17-Q2 [single] Blended learning combines face-to-face and online learning; the flipped classroom (content online first, class time for application) is one blended model; rotation and flex models are blended but not flipped.
- S18-Q28 [scenario] Synchronous components require everyone present at the same moment (live lecture); forums, recorded lectures and quizzes open over a time window are asynchronous.
- S19-Q39 [assertion-reason] Blended learning extends learning time because online activities can be done outside the timetable; the flipped classroom is one blended model, but that classification does not explain the extension.
- S20-Q38 [single] A threaded discussion forum supports extended reasoned discussion over days without simultaneous presence (asynchronous); a webinar is synchronous; podcasts and broadcast e-mail are one-way.

### Going Further must teach
- S4-Q12 [single] Jonathan Bergmann and Aaron Sams, US chemistry teachers, began recording lectures in 2007 (book Flip Your Classroom). Hase and Kenyon = heutagogy; Siemens and Downes = connectivism; Joyce and Weil = models of teaching.
- S8-Q1 [scenario] Blended learning models (Christensen Institute): rotation (move on schedule between online and other modes, e.g. station rotation), flex (online backbone), enriched virtual (mostly remote), self-blend/a la carte (student adds online courses).
- S14-Q14 [scenario] In a flipped course, re-lecturing for unprepared students removes the incentive to prepare; a brief entry quiz or task holds students accountable while the planned in-class activity proceeds.
- S20-Q27 [single] Moore: transactional distance is psychological and depends on dialogue, structure and learner autonomy; high structure with low dialogue gives greatest distance; more dialogue reduces it.
