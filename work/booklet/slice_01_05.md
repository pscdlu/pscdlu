### S1-Q1  [key A]
In Continuous and Comprehensive Evaluation, the word 'comprehensive' means covering:

(A)	both scholastic and co-scholastic areas

(B)	both written and oral examinations

(C)	both internal and external examiners

(D)	both school and college education
EXPLANATION: (A),** because in CCE, 'continuous' refers to regular assessment throughout the year and 'comprehensive' to coverage of both scholastic areas (academic subjects) and co-scholastic areas such as life skills, attitudes, values, arts and sports. The scheme sought to reduce dependence on a single year-end examination.

### S1-Q2  [key C]
The project method of teaching was developed by:

(A) Maria Montessori	(B) Friedrich Froebel

(C) W. H. Kilpatrick	(D) Helen Parkhurst
EXPLANATION: (C),** because William Heard Kilpatrick set out the project method in 1918, building on Dewey's idea of learning by doing. Its steps are purposing, planning, executing and evaluating. Montessori and Froebel are known for early childhood methods, and Parkhurst for the Dalton Plan.

### S1-Q3  [key A]
Teaching at the understanding level aims mainly at:

(A)	grasping the relationships among facts and principles

(B)	memorising facts and definitions for later recall

(C)	solving open problems through independent inquiry

(D)	practising physical skills until they are automatic
EXPLANATION: (A),** because the understanding level goes beyond memorising to seeing meaning: how facts relate to one another and to general principles, so that the learner can explain and apply them in familiar situations. Rote recall belongs to the memory level and independent problem-solving to the reflective level.

### S1-Q4  [key C]
A small-group session in which a teacher attends to the individual difficulties of students is called a:

(A) lecture	(B) symposium

(C) tutorial	(D) conference
EXPLANATION: (C),** because a tutorial brings together a small group of students with a tutor who clears doubts, checks understanding and gives personal feedback. It supplements lectures. A lecture addresses a large group, while a symposium and a conference involve formal presentations by several speakers.

### S1-Q5  [key C]
Micro-teaching was first developed at:

(A) Harvard University	(B) Columbia University

(C) Stanford University	(D) Oxford University
EXPLANATION: (C),** because micro-teaching was developed at Stanford University in 1963 by Dwight W. Allen and his colleagues as a way of giving trainee teachers controlled practice in single teaching skills with immediate feedback. It was later adopted in Indian teacher education in a modified form.

### S1-Q6  [key D]
In the revised Bloom's taxonomy (Anderson and Krathwohl, 2001), the highest cognitive level is:

(A) Evaluate	(B) Analyse

(C) Apply	(D) Create
EXPLANATION: (D),** because the revised taxonomy uses verbs and runs Remember, Understand, Apply, Analyse, Evaluate, Create. Create, which means putting elements together to form something new, sits at the top. In the original 1956 version the highest level was Evaluation, with Synthesis just below it.

### S1-Q7  [key B]
A test that interprets a student's score by comparing it with the scores of other students is:

(A) criterion-referenced	(B) norm-referenced

(C) self-referenced	(D) content-referenced
EXPLANATION: (B),** because a norm-referenced test reports where a student stands relative to a group, using ranks or percentiles; competitive selection tests work this way. A criterion-referenced test compares performance with a fixed standard, regardless of how other students perform.

### S1-Q8  [key A]
The concept of the Zone of Proximal Development was given by:

(A) Lev Vygotsky	(B) Jean Piaget

(C) Jerome Bruner	(D) David Ausubel
EXPLANATION: (A),** because Vygotsky defined the Zone of Proximal Development as the gap between what a learner can do alone and what the learner can do with help from a teacher or a more capable peer. Teaching is most effective when it is aimed at this zone and gives suitable support.

### S1-Q9  [key D]
A test that gives consistent results when administered again under similar conditions is said to be:

(A) valid	(B) comprehensive

(C) economical	(D) reliable
EXPLANATION: (D),** because reliability is the consistency or stability of test scores. If the same students get similar scores on repeated administration, the test is reliable. Validity is a different quality: it asks whether the test measures what it claims to measure. A good test needs both.

### S1-Q10  [key D]
The term 'andragogy', meaning the teaching of adults, was popularised by:

(A) Jean Piaget	(B) B. F. Skinner

(C) Benjamin Bloom	(D) Malcolm Knowles
EXPLANATION: (D),** because Malcolm Knowles popularised andragogy as the art and science of helping adults learn. He described adult learners as self-directed, rich in experience, ready to learn what their life roles require, problem-centred and internally motivated. Pedagogy, by contrast, refers to the teaching of children.

### S1-Q11  [key B]
The lecture method is generally classified as a:

(A) learner-centred method	(B) teacher-centred method

(C) activity-centred method	(D) problem-centred method
EXPLANATION: (B),** because in a lecture the teacher does most of the talking and controls content and pace, while learners mainly listen and take notes. It is therefore teacher-centred. It is economical for large classes and useful for introducing topics, but it gives little room for learner activity.

### S1-Q12  [key B]
The taxonomy of the affective domain is chiefly associated with:

(A) E. J. Simpson	(B) D. R. Krathwohl

(C) R. H. Dave	(D) R. M. Gagné
EXPLANATION: (B),** because Krathwohl, with Bloom and Masia, published the affective domain taxonomy in 1964, with levels running from Receiving to Characterisation. The affective domain concerns attitudes, values and feelings. Simpson and Dave are associated with the psychomotor domain, and Gagné with the conditions of learning.

### S1-Q13  [key A]
A teacher who critically examines her own classroom practice in order to improve it is showing:

(A) reflective practice	(B) authoritarian control

(C) verbal fluency	(D) subject mastery
EXPLANATION: (A),** because reflective practice means looking back on one's own teaching, asking what worked and what did not, and changing future lessons accordingly. Subject mastery and verbal fluency are also desirable qualities, but they describe what a teacher knows or how she speaks, not self-examination.

### S1-Q14  [key C]
A test used to locate the specific learning difficulties of a student is a:

(A) prognostic test	(B) aptitude test

(C) diagnostic test	(D) intelligence test
EXPLANATION: (C),** because a diagnostic test identifies exactly where and why a learner is going wrong, so that remedial teaching can be planned. A prognostic test predicts future success, an aptitude test measures potential for a particular field, and an intelligence test measures general mental ability.

### S1-Q15  [key B]

EXPLANATION: (B),** because as class size grows, the teacher has less time for each student, so individual guidance, interaction and feedback suffer. A lecture, an announcement or a common written test can still be given to a large group, which is why the lecture dominates large classes.

### S1-Q16  [key A]

(A) Visual	(B) Verbal

EXPLANATION: (A),** because VARK, developed by Neil Fleming, stands for Visual, Auditory (or Aural), Read/Write and Kinaesthetic. Visual learners prefer diagrams, charts and maps. The practical message for teachers is to present material in varied ways, since a class contains learners with different preferences.

### S1-Q17  [key D]
Brainstorming is a technique used mainly to:

(A)	memorise a large number of facts quickly

(B)	test a large number of students together

(C)	revise a large number of topics briefly

EXPLANATION: (D),** because brainstorming, introduced by Alex Osborn, asks a group to produce as many ideas as possible on a problem without criticism at first; evaluation comes later. It encourages creative thinking and participation. It is not a technique for memorising, testing or revising content.

### S1-Q18  [key B]
The theory of multiple intelligences was proposed by:

(A) Charles Spearman	(B) Howard Gardner

(C) Alfred Binet	(D) Raymond Cattell
EXPLANATION: (B),** because Howard Gardner, in Frames of Mind (1983), argued that intelligence is not a single ability but several relatively independent ones, such as linguistic, logical-mathematical, spatial, musical, bodily-kinaesthetic, interpersonal and intrapersonal. Spearman proposed a general factor, Binet built the first intelligence test, and Cattell distinguished fluid from crystallised intelligence.

### S1-Q19  [key C]

(A) Instruction	(B) Training

(C) Indoctrination	(D) Tutoring
EXPLANATION: (C),** because indoctrination implants beliefs as unquestionable and discourages critical examination, which is why it is regarded as anti-educational. Instruction delivers structured content, training builds specific skills through practice, and tutoring gives individual help; all three leave room for understanding and questioning.

### S1-Q20  [key B]

### S1-Q21  [key A]

(A) Edgar Dale	(B) Jerome Bruner

(C) Robert Gagné	(D) Benjamin Bloom
EXPLANATION: (A),** because Edgar Dale (1946) arranged learning experiences in a cone, with direct purposeful experience at the broad base and verbal symbols at the narrow top. The cone reminds teachers that concrete experiences support abstract ideas, and it guides the choice of teaching aids.

### S1-Q22  [key B]

EXPLANATION: (B),** because handling apparatus is a physical skill that depends on coordination between mind and muscle, so it belongs to the psychomotor domain. The cognitive domain concerns thinking, the affective domain concerns attitudes and values, and conation refers to will or striving, not to manual skill.

### S1-Q24  [key D]

### S1-Q43  [key B]

### S5-Q50  [key C]
The person who conducts a panel discussion and keeps it on track is the:

(A) rapporteur	(B) respondent

(C) moderator	(D) invigilator
EXPLANATION: (C),** because the moderator introduces the topic and the panel, puts questions, gives each member a fair chance, prevents digression and sums up. A rapporteur records the proceedings and prepares the report. An invigilator supervises an examination.
