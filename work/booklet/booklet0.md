#### How to use this booklet

This booklet comes before the ten unit booklets. It holds five papers of equal difficulty. Each paper has 50 questions, five from every unit of the Paper 1 syllabus, in the mix of formats you will meet in the examination. Its purpose is simple: to show you where you stand today and which units need your attention first.

**1. Take Paper 1 first, before you open any unit booklet.** Sit in a quiet place, set a timer for 60 minutes and answer all 50 questions at one sitting. There is no negative marking, so leave nothing blank.
**2. Do not look at the key until the time is up.** A paper checked halfway through tells you nothing about your level.
**3. Mark it honestly.** Give yourself 1 for each correct answer, using the Answer Key that follows the paper. If you guessed and happened to be right, count it, but put a small mark against the question so that you read its explanation.
**4. Fill in the Score Sheet.** It asks for your total out of 50 and your score out of 5 in each unit. The unit scores matter as much as the total.
**5. Read the explanations.** Read them for every question you got wrong or guessed. For the harder questions, the explanation also says why the most tempting wrong option is wrong.
**6. Find your place.** Turn to “Where do I stand?” for the meaning of your total, and to “Which booklet should I start with?” for your order of study.
**7. Come back for the other four papers.** Take them one at a time over the following weeks, for example after every two unit booklets, under the same conditions. All five papers follow one blueprint, so your scores can be compared and your progress tracked. Two things to know. Every question and passage in this booklet is newly written for practice. And where the examination numbers its options 1 to 4, this booklet letters them (A) to (D) and numbers the statements inside a question; nothing else about the formats differs.

*Read the passage and answer Questions 46 to 50.* Peer review, the practice of submitting scholarly work to the scrutiny of other specialists before publication, is often described as the gatekeeper of science. The description flatters it. Reviewers are unpaid volunteers who read a manuscript once or twice, usually without access to the underlying data, and they are asked to judge in a few hours what took the authors years to produce. Studies in which deliberately flawed papers were sent to reviewers have found that most of the planted errors went unnoticed. Peer review is also slow, and it can be conservative: unconventional ideas that challenge a field's assumptions tend to face a rougher passage than work that extends what reviewers already believe. Why, then, does the system survive? One reason is that its critics have rarely agreed on a replacement. A second and more important reason is that peer review does more than detect error. A reviewer's questions force authors to clarify their methods, temper their claims and acknowledge rival explanations. Editors who compare submitted and published versions of the same paper routinely report that the published version is more cautious and more clearly argued. In this sense, review works less like a customs check at a border and more like a conversation that improves what passes through it.

UGC NET Paper 1 · Booklet 0 · Where Do I Stand? · Page 16 of 93

The real danger lies in what readers outside the academy take peer review to mean. Journalists and policymakers frequently treat the phrase 'peer-reviewed study' as a guarantee of truth. It is nothing of the kind. Publication signals only that a small number of informed readers found the work worth debating; whether its findings hold is settled later, when other researchers try to reproduce them. A claim earns trust through replication, not through the stamp of a journal. Recent reforms accept this modest view. Some journals now publish reviewers' reports alongside articles, so that readers can see what was questioned. Others invite comment after publication, treating review as a continuing process rather than a single verdict. Such measures do not make peer review infallible. They make its limits visible, which may be the more honest achievement.

Concrete and asphalt absorb sunlight through the day and release it slowly after dark, while vehicles, air conditioners and factories add heat of their own. The difference, known as the urban heat island effect, can reach several degrees on a summer night, and it is felt most sharply in dense neighbourhoods with little open ground. Trees are the most frequently proposed remedy, and the reasoning is sound. A mature tree shades the surfaces beneath it and cools the air around it by releasing water vapour through its leaves. Streets lined with large trees are measurably cooler than bare ones. Yet planting drives announced with great publicity often achieve less than their numbers suggest. Saplings are counted on the day they are planted, not five years later, and in many cities fewer than half survive without regular watering and protection. Surveys in several countries have found that tree cover follows wealth: affluent districts tend to have broad, shaded avenues, while poorer and more crowded quarters, where residents are least able to afford cooling, have the fewest trees. Planting is easiest where pavements are wide and land is available, which is rarely where it is most needed. *Read the passage and answer Questions 46 to 50.* For much of the last two centuries, historians trusted paper above memory. A document, they reasoned, is fixed at the moment of its writing, whereas a recollection is reshaped every time it is told. Oral testimony was accordingly treated as a source of last resort, useful for colour but unreliable for fact. That confidence has weakened, for two reasons. The first is a recognition of what archives leave out. Official records are produced by institutions for their own purposes: a revenue office records what was assessed and collected, not what a bad harvest meant to the family that paid. People who could not write, or whom no office thought worth describing, appear in such records only as numbers, if they appear at all. To write the history of agricultural labourers, domestic workers or migrants from documents alone is to see them through the eyes of those who governed them. Interviews with such people, or with their children, do not merely add detail; they bring into view experiences that left no paper behind. The second reason is a more careful understanding of memory itself. Oral historians concede that people misremember dates, merge separate events and tell their stories differently to different listeners. But they argue that these distortions are themselves evidence. When many people in a town 'remember' a strike as having happened in the wrong year, the error may show what the strike had come to mean for them, and which later event it had become tied to. The question shifts from whether the account is accurate to why it is told in this way. This does not make testimony self-sufficient. A careful researcher sets interviews beside documents and against one another, and notes who is speaking, to whom, and how long after the events described. The point is that the same scrutiny must be applied to the written record. A document is also composed by someone, for an audience, with an interest in how matters appear. Oral history, at its best, has not replaced the archive; it has taught historians to read the archive with less innocence.

*Read the passage and answer Questions 46 to 50.* Governments have traditionally changed behaviour with two instruments: rules that forbid or require, and prices that reward or penalise. In the last two decades a third has attracted attention. A 'nudge' alters the way a choice is presented without removing any option or changing its cost. The best-known example concerns pension savings. Where employees must fill in a form to join a scheme, many never do; where they are enrolled automatically and must fill in a form to leave, most stay. Nothing has been forbidden and no one has been paid, yet participation rises sharply. The appeal of such measures is obvious. They are cheap, they leave people free to choose otherwise, and they work with human habits rather than against them. People tend to accept whatever is set as the default, to put off tasks that require effort and to follow what they believe others do. A tax office that tells late payers that nine out of ten of their neighbours have already paid collects more than one that merely repeats the deadline. Critics raise two objections. The first is about consent. A nudge works best when it is not noticed, and a policy that depends on people not noticing how they are being steered sits uneasily with the idea that citizens should be reasoned with. Defenders reply that there is no neutral way to present a choice: some option must come first on the form, and some arrangement must hold if a person does nothing. Since a default is unavoidable, it may as well be chosen with care and declared openly.

The second objection is about scale. The effects of nudges, though real, are usually modest, and they fade when the prompt is withdrawn. Problems such as low retirement savings or high energy use have causes, such as low wages and poor housing, that no rearrangement of a form will touch. The risk is not that nudges fail but that their low cost makes them attractive substitutes for costlier measures that would achieve more. A nudge is a useful supplement to policy.
